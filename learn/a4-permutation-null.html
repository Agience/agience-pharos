Entroptics / lesson four · the null

Is There
Anything There?

The first three units all read a record whose rows were in a meaningful order — time, mostly — and every one of them assumed that order was real. None of them checked.

This unit asks the question they skipped, and the honest answer turns out to be computable.

Two records, both already familiar: the EEG from unit A3, and the same closing prices unit A1 opened with.

The two records

record A — the EEG. 800 readings from 4 electrodes, taken one after another. The rows are ordered in time and we have every reason to believe that matters.

record B — the market. 71 months of closing prices for 8 companies. Also ordered in time.

Both look ordered. The question is whether the order carries anything a measurement can find — and “it obviously does” is not a measurement.

The honest test

If the order matters, then destroying the order should destroy something you can measure. If nothing changes, the order was never carrying anything.

So: take the record and shuffle the rows into a random sequence. Every row is still there. Every number is still there. Each electrode still has exactly the same 800 readings in it. The only thing gone is which came after which.

Then measure both, and compare.

This is the same move unit A5 makes on symbol sequences. It is the single most reusable idea in the whole curriculum: to find out whether something matters, remove it and see what breaks.

And it does break

From +41.29 to -1.05. Not smaller — gone, and slightly negative, which is what a number centred on zero does when it lands on the low side.

Nothing was removed except the order, so whatever the read was seeing, it was seeing the order. That is what a z-score is for: it says how far a measurement sits from what you would get if the thing you care about were not there, counted in units of ordinary wobble. Two is the usual line. +41.29 is not close to the line.

You never have to shuffle

The obvious way to get that comparison is to shuffle a thousand times and see where the real record lands. That works, it is slow, and it gives a slightly different answer every run because it depends on the shuffles you happened to draw.

You do not have to. Start with what is actually being measured — how alike, on average, each row is to the one after it:

A  =  meani  Re ⟨ rowi , rowi+1 ⟩2

⟨ a , b ⟩ is the inner product — multiply the two rows entry by entry and add the results up. Squaring it drops the sign, so two rows that move oppositely count as much as two that move together — what is being asked is whether consecutive rows are related, in either direction. meani averages that over every neighbouring pair in the record.

And because μ and the spread are built from the same squared products, multiplying the whole record by a constant leaves z exactly where it was. One row made louder does move it, which is right: that row really has become less like its neighbours.

Now the key step. Under a random shuffle, each compared pair becomes a uniformly random pair of distinct rows. So the average value of A over all possible shuffles is just the average over all row pairs — and you can compute that directly, from the record you already have, without drawing a single shuffle.

The library calls the exact average μ — the Greek letter mu, said MEW, the standard name for a mean. The spread around it has an exact formula too (the Cliff–Ord / Mantel second moment), which matters because neighbouring pairs share a row and so are not independent.

The z-score, in full

z  =  ( A  −  μ )  ÷  √Var

Three pieces, and every one of them is computed from the record itself:

  • A — what the record actually scores.
  • μ (mu) — what it would score on average if the rows were in a random order. Exact, not sampled.
  • √Var — the square root of the variance, which is the ordinary spread of A across shuffles. √ is the square root sign. Also exact.

So z answers: how many ordinary wobbles above the no-order average is this record? No random number generator is involved anywhere, which means the same record gives the same z every time, on every machine.

The exactness is the whole point. A shuffled null is an estimate of a thing that has a closed form — and paying a thousand shuffles for a worse version of a number you could have computed is a bad trade.

Four verdicts

The EEG is ordered, overwhelmingly: +41.29.

The market's prices are ordered too, at +9.30 — but for a duller reason than it looks. A share price does not jump; today's is near yesterday's. The read is picking up that neighbouring rows are simply close together.

The month-to-month changes read +0.127. Subtract the level and the order stops carrying anything the instrument can find. That is the honest answer, and it is the same one unit A5 reaches from a completely different direction — reduced to up/flat/down symbols, the same prices gave redundancy exactly 0.0000.

Two instruments that share no code path, agreeing on the same record. That agreement is worth more than either reading alone.

But can you trust the null?

A z-score is only meaningful if it really is centred on zero with a spread of one when there is nothing there. So feed the instrument pure noise — 400 draws of it at each of several shapes, with no structure of any kind — and check.

Centred, spread one, and the share landing above 2 sits between 0.0175 and 0.0400 against a target of 0.0228 — across shapes from 4 columns to 200.

The small excess in the last column is real and understood: the standardisation is exact, but the shape of the tail is not quite a bell curve at these sizes. The library's own exp4_coherence.py runs 2000 draws — 400 at each of five shapes — and measures 0.0265. That figure is not quoted from a docstring: this unit's audit imports that script, runs it, and fails if the number moves.

A calibration check needs enough draws to calibrate, and this table does not. At 400 draws per shape the share still swings from 0.0175 to 0.0400 — a spread of 0.0225 around a target of 0.0228, where the excess it would take to see is 0.0265 − 0.0228 = 0.0037. The spread is 6 times the quantity being measured. The excess is real; reading it takes the library's 2,000 draws. This table is here to show the z-score is centred and spread one, which 400 draws is ample for.

The same question, asked of the null you actually use

That table fed the instrument synthetic noise. The null this unit is built on is a shuffle of the real record, which keeps every row exactly as it was and destroys only the order. Those are two different nulls, and there is no reason in advance for them to have the same tail. So: 2000 shuffles of the EEG, each one through the whole instrument.

mean0+0.0335

μ and Var hold. A mean that lands on zero and a spread that lands on one, with nothing fitted — measured from outside the library, on the null the theorem is about. That is the closed form of the previous slides earning its keep.

The tail is a different story, and the numbers stack up:

share above z = 2
a bell curve0.0228
synthetic noise, the library's 2,000 draws0.0265
shuffles of this record, 2000 draws0.0515

Shuffling a real record puts 0.0515 of the draws above z = 2 — 1.9× the synthetic-noise share and 2.3× a bell curve's. Of 2000 shuffles, 103 land above +2 and 1 below −2: the null is centred, correctly scaled, and distinctly right-leaning.

Which is worth stating plainly, because it is the practical content of this whole unit: μ and Var pin the centre and the spread; the tail is a separate measurement. The two moments are exact and cheap and you should use them — and on this record's null a z of 2 is a one in 19 event, where a bell curve puts it at one in 44.

And it leaves the record itself untouched. The EEG scores +41.29 — 8.1× the loudest of 2000 shuffles constructed to contain nothing. A tail twice as heavy as expected does not begin to reach it.

At what zoom does it appear?

One more question the earlier units never asked: how much of the record do you have to look at before the structure shows up? Read the same EEG through a sliding window and vary the window's width. It turns out two different questions give two different answers.

Coherence is already 2.68 through a window of just 8 rows — above the line on the very first try. Something is ordered, and you can tell from a tiny slice.

But asking to actually resolve a mode — to pull out a distinct pattern that stands above the noise floor, which is what units A1 to A3 were counting — is a harder question, and it needs a wider window:

Nothing resolves until 28 rows. The library reports exactly that as the resolved window: 28 rows — the smallest window at which one mode first stands above the floor. Strongest coherence is at 800 rows: the whole record.

Look at windows 65 and 99, where the count falls back to 0 after having been 1. Widening the window admits more signal and more noise, and at those two widths the floor rises faster than the mode does. The staircase is not monotonic, and the instrument reports the shape it measures.

Same idea as the entropy-matched scale in unit A1, made literal. There it set how finely to divide an axis; here it sets how much axis you need before a question can be answered at all.

Order, measured

Every read in units A1 to A3 rests on the ordered axis being ordered. That is now a measurement, and it is free: no sampling, no seed, no run-to-run variation, one pass over the record.

recordzverdict
EEG, as recorded+41.29ordered
market, price levels+9.30ordered — but only because prices drift slowly
market, monthly change+0.127nothing found
EEG, rows shuffled-1.05nothing found

The shuffled EEG row is the one that matters most. It is a negative control: a case where you already know the answer, run through the same instrument, to check the instrument can read zero. A read that returns a number on every input measures nothing.

One shuffle is one draw. The row above is a single permutation, and 2000 of them have mean +0.0335 and never exceed +5.10 — a null whose shape turns out to matter more than its centre.
