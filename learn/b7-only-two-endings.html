Entroptics / lesson B7 · the two fates of a finite decay

Only Two Endings

Build a decay out of a finite number of pieces, where each piece keeps its own fixed fraction of itself at every step. Two endings are available to that decay, and this unit measures both of them to nine decimals.

C(τ) = P1m1τ + P2m2τ + … + PFmFτ

Reading it. τ, said tau, counts steps. m is the fraction one piece keeps each step, and P is how big that piece started. A piece with m = 0.9 keeps 0.9 of itself after one step and 0.81 after two.

Those are the two. The third shape anyone might reach for — a slow slide where the decay falls by half over each doubling of τ — is measured on the fourth slide, and the measurement shows what it costs.

Unit B6 measured the constant that turns a read width into a gap. This one measures where a gap comes from. Both are one step of the same argument, and the D5 units that come later will use them together.

Ending one: every piece under one

Six pieces, the largest keeping 0.951927257 of itself each step. Add them up and watch what fraction the whole sum keeps from one step to the next:

The sum ends up keeping exactly what its largest piece keeps — 0.951926843 against 0.951927257, agreeing to 7 digits. The five smaller pieces shrink faster, so by τ = 2000 they have gone from the total and the largest one is what is left.

rate = −log(0.951927257) = 0.04926666 per step

That rate is the gap. A decay that falls by a fixed fraction every step has a single number describing it, and every finite sum whose pieces all sit under 1 arrives here. The size of the gap is set by the largest piece, and the rest of the sum decides how long it takes to show.

The six pieces run from 0.800974 to 0.951927257. Sums with a wider spread settle sooner, and sums whose top two pieces sit close together take longer — the arithmetic is the same and the τ at which it is visible moves.

0.700000000000, and it holds there. The six shrinking pieces fade out as before, and what remains is the piece that keeps everything: its weight, exactly, for as long as anyone cares to look.

This is the gapless ending. A piece at m = 1 carries content that survives every step, so the correlation reaches out to any distance at full strength. In the physics this unit serves, that is a massless mode, and its reach is what makes it one.

The two endings are settled by one comparison: is the largest m below 1, or at 1? Below 1 gives a rate. At 1 gives a floor. A finite sum of these pieces arrives at one of the two.

The shape in between

The third shape is a power law: the decay falls by a fixed fraction over each doubling of τ, so it stays alive far longer than a fixed fraction per step. Measure both shapes two ways — how fast each falls per step, and how fast each falls per doubling:

Each shape holds one column still, and the green cells are those columns. The power law keeps -2.000000 per doubling at every τ. The finite sum keeps -0.04926666 per step at every τ, which is the rate the second slide measured.

The other column tells the same story from the other side. The finite sum's per-doubling figure runs 10.2, 39.6, 157.7 — it grows with τ and keeps growing. Holding that column still across all τ is what a power law requires, and a sum of finitely many pieces produces the growing column every time.

This is the step the mass-gap argument turns on. A theory with no gap needs the power law. The power law needs a column that a finite sum sends off to infinity, so a finite instrument reading a finite record lands on one of the two endings above.

The test, on a record with a known answer

Two flat columns make a test: measure both slopes on a record, and whichever holds still says which shape the record has. Run it first where the answer was put in by hand — a record built so that it falls at 0.05 per step:

The per-step column returns the rate that was built in — 0.05, read as -0.050851, -0.052881 and -0.050717, a worst miss of 0.002881. The per-doubling column doubles as the lag doubles, which is the growing column from the previous slide seen on real data.

Where the read stops working, measured. At lag 80 the correlation has fallen to 0.008030, and the per-step slope reads -0.117203 against the 0.05 that was planted. A correlation that small carries mostly the record's own scatter, so the reading widens. Unit A13 measures exactly how much of a decay is that scatter, and it is the reading to take alongside this one.

The usable range is the part of the curve where C is still large enough to carry the signal, and the honest way to find it is to build a record whose answer is known and see where the test stops returning it. That is what this slide is.

The per-step column holds within a factor of 1.606 across that range (-0.034303 to -0.055102). The per-doubling column spreads by 3.356 over the same lags (-0.3275 to -1.0993) and grows steadily with the lag.

So the per-step reading is the steadier of the two, by 2.09 times, which places this hillside with the first ending: a decay that falls by a fixed fraction per step, at a rate reading between 0.034 and 0.055 across these lags. The elevation of two points a fixed distance apart is related by a fixed factor, whatever part of the hill they sit on.

A factor of 1.606 is a real spread and it is reported as one. The built record on the previous slide held its column to a worst miss of 0.002881, which is what a clean single-piece decay looks like; this hillside sits looser than that and still separates clearly from the growing column beside it.

The argument in one line. An instrument that reads a finite record sees finitely many pieces. Finitely many pieces give one of the first two rows. The third row is the one a gapless theory needs, and reaching it takes a number of pieces that a finite screen has no room for.

What a reader can do with this. Take any record, form its decay, and measure both slopes across a range where C is still large. Whichever column holds still names the shape, and if the per-step column is the steady one, that reading is the rate. Unit B6 supplies the constant that turns a rate into a stated gap, and unit D5c comes later to run both on a real field.

Every number on the first four slides is arithmetic on a sum written by hand, so they carry as many digits as anyone cares to print. The fifth and sixth slides run the same test on records, where the answer arrives with a width, and both slides report that width.
