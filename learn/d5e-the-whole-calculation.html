Lines 1 to 3: count the surfaces

A vortex is a closed sheet made of square faces. Take the simplest family of them: solids built by stacking cubes, each cube touching the last face to face, always stepping in +x, +y or +z. The outside of such a solid is a closed sheet, so every one of them counts.

Line 1 — one cube. Six faces, nothing to hide any of them: A = 6.

Line 2 — each further cube. It brings six new faces, and the face where it meets its neighbour goes inside, which removes that face from both cubes. Six on, two off, so four each time:

A(n)  =  6n − 2(n−1)  =  4n + 2

Line 3 — how many. A stack of n cubes is decided by its n−1 steps, and each step is a free choice among three:

N(n)  =  3n−1

Lines 4 and 5: three choices over four faces

Three choices arrive together with four faces, so ask what one face is worth. Spread the three evenly over the four:

κ0 = 0.274653 is a rate: how fast the number of sheets grows for every face of area. Physics writes growth as a rate because rates add along a path where factors multiply, and the natural logarithm is the tool that converts between the two.

Line 5 turns the rate back into a factor, which is the form the measured tables are read against. A growth rate of κ0 means a sheet's weight has to shrink by at least the same rate to stay outnumbered:

Check it the third way if you like: four of them multiplied together, 0.759836 × 0.759836 × 0.759836 × 0.759836, give 0.333334 — which is 1 ÷ 3. Four faces of shrinking exactly undo one three-way choice, which is what the counting said.

This number carries no physics. It came from stacking cubes. No coupling appears in it, no lattice spacing, nothing measured — which is what lets it stand as a floor at every coupling at once.

Lines 6 and 7: what one plaquette costs

The other side of the inequality is μ, the cost of one face of vortex sheet. It comes from the aperture: a screen only resolves angles down to a finite step, and that step sets how far the field can turn between neighbouring points.

Line 6 — the aperture. A screen of side L resolves angles in steps of 2π/(L+1). That is unit A9's finding in one line: a finite window resolves finite detail, and a wider window resolves finer.

Line 7 — the cost. The tension is an average of cosines, and a cosine is never below the parabola 1 − x²/2, so replacing it with the parabola can only make the bound looser. Then −log turns the average into a rate. Working at L = 12 with the proof's uniform input ⟨d²⟩ ≤ 1:

Line 8: the answer

The floor is what the sheets gain from being numerous. The cost is what one of them pays. The gap is what is left over:

Δ  ≥  0.150449  >  0

A positive number is the whole result. The claim was that the decay rate of the field's correlations has a floor above zero, and eight lines of arithmetic put one there. The size of the number matters less than its sign: a gap of any positive size means the correlations die off at a definite rate, and a rate is what a mass is.

What each line contributed. Line 4 is geometry — it would read 0.274653 in any theory built on this lattice. Line 7 is the theory — it is where the gauge field, its coupling and its aperture enter. Line 8 is the comparison, and it is the only place the two meet.

The check: what the instrument measured
