The Constant in Front

An instrument reads a width. A physicist asks for a gap. One number stands between them, and that number is fixed by the shape of the decay alone — the same way a telescope's resolution carries a 1.22 that belongs to the circle alone.

Δ = g × aδ

Reading the symbols. The width an instrument reports is written aδ, said a-delta. A decay is written against τ, said tau, which counts how far apart along the record two points sit. The tick mark √ in the table below is a square root, and e is the number 2.718…, the one that turns up whenever something falls by the same fraction each step.

Unit B5 measured aδ on eight records. This unit measures g. It is one line of the mass-gap argument, and the four D5 units that come later will each use it. Here it is worked out.

The first six slides hand the instrument an exact shape and read the constant straight back. Working from the shape itself is what lets these numbers arrive to six decimals; the seventh slide measures what it takes to recover one from real data.

A shape whose constant is one

Start with the simplest decay there is: the correlation holds at full strength out to a width W, and beyond W it is finished. Two lengths can be read off it. ξ is how far the correlation reaches, added up. aδ is the width the instrument reports. Multiply them:

Waδξ ξ × aδ
500.02000050.01.0000000000000004
1000.010000100.01.0000000000000013
2000.005000200.01.0000000000000009

One, at every width, to fifteen decimal places. Double W and aδ halves, ξ doubles, and the product holds. The instrument is reporting exactly the reach that is there.

This is the anchor for everything that follows. A shape whose constant is 1 is a shape where the reported width is the reach. Every other shape is read against this one.

The instrument publishes this product as rayleigh_shape_factor. Unit B5 checked that it equals xi * a_delta on two real records, agreeing to nine decimals. Here the same product is evaluated on a shape whose answer can be worked out by hand.

The same number at every width

Now a bell-shaped decay, exp(−(τ÷s)2), measured at three widths. The width s runs from 20 to 80 — a factor of 4. Watch aδ follow it, and watch the last column hold still:

saδ 1 ÷ (s × aδ)
200.024197072.066366
400.012098542.066366
800.006049272.066366

2.066366, three times, to every decimal shown. aδ moved by a factor of four, and the constant held at every decimal shown.

This is what makes it a shape factor. It carries the shape, and it holds at every size. Give the instrument a bell twice as wide and it reports a width twice as large, and the number connecting the two is the same number. That is why a single measurement of g is worth having: once, for a shape, and it holds for every record with that shape.

Three widths, because one width shows a number and two show a coincidence. A quantity that claims to ignore the scale gets tested across the scale.

The plain decay gives e

A decay that falls by the same fraction every step — exp(−Dτ) — is the shape a gap produces. D is the gap. Hand the shape to the instrument and divide:

e = 2.718282

2.718191 against 2.718282 — agreement to 0.0033%. The number that turns this instrument's width into a gap is e, the base of the natural logarithm, arriving out of a measurement handed one decay curve.

Why this row is the one that matters. A ladder of evenly spaced energy levels, warm, decays exactly this way. So for the system the mass-gap argument is about, the gap is the instrument's width times e. Unit D5c reads aδ off a real field; this is the number it is multiplied by.

The agreement is closest at the smallest D and widens as D grows: at D = 0.10 the decay falls by 9.5% in a single step, and a curve read at whole steps carries that coarseness. The constant belongs to the smooth shape, and the finer the sampling the closer the measurement sits.

Write the shape two ways, and the constant moves

Here is the same bell, written the two ways it is usually written. Both are Gaussians. Both call their width s:

2.922282 ÷ 2.066366 = 1.41421356   ·   √2 = 1.41421356

The ratio is √2, to eight decimals. The two formulas describe one curve at two different values of s: putting a 2 under the square changes what the letter s names, and the constant carries that change exactly.

So a shape factor travels with its convention. Quote g and quote the formula the width was defined by, and any reader reproduces the number. Both rows above are correct, and they are answers to two different questions about the same bell.

Every entry in the mass-gap paper's shape table appears in this measurement: 1, 2.066366, 2.922282, and 4.132731 — each one √2 from its neighbour. Which one a calculation uses follows from how that calculation writes its width.

This is the telescope's 1.22

Every optics text gives the resolution of a round telescope as

θ = 1.22 × λ ÷ D

θ, said theta, is the angle between two points just far enough apart to arrive as two. λ, said lambda, is the wavelength of the light, and D is the width of the opening.

and the 1.22 is a shape factor. It belongs to the circle. Light through a round opening lands in a bright disc whose first dark ring sits where a particular function first reaches zero, and the constant is that zero divided by π:

first zero of J13.8317059702

1.22 is a measured constant, and this is the measurement. A square opening gives a different number. A slit gives a different number again. The wavelength and the aperture size sit in the formula as λ and D; the 1.22 is the circle, on its own.

The four constants on the previous slides are the same kind of number. A decay shape stands where the circle stands, aδ stands where λ÷D stands, and g stands where 1.22 stands. Unit B5 called aδ the oldest number in optics; this is the constant that has always travelled in front of it.

Taking it to a record

The six slides above hand the instrument an exact shape. A real measurement hands it a record, and the record's decay is estimated from the columns it carries. Here is the same plain decay, D = 0.05, built as a record and read back at six column counts:

The reading settles as the columns arrive: 4.8472 at 4 columns, 1.5882 at 64, and 1.4995 by 1024, where it has been stable for two doublings.

The instrument states this requirement itself. Its documentation gives the numbers for a record whose answer is exactly 1: it returns 0.43, 0.70, 0.90, 0.97 and 0.99 at 4, 16, 64, 256 and 1024 columns. The decay is summed across the columns, so the scatter left in it falls as the count rises, and a wider record buys a tighter read.

This slide exists because the first attempt at this unit used four columns and read the plain decay's constant as 4.85. Unit A12 counts how many columns of a record are working and unit A13 measures how much of a decay is the record's own scatter. Both of those are the reading to take before this one.

A shape factor is exact and a measurement of it is a measurement. The exact constants sit on the slides above, taken from the shape; this table is what it costs to recover one from data.

Where it goes next. A warm ladder of evenly spaced levels decays by the fourth row, so its gap is e times the width the instrument reads. That single sentence is what units D5c, D5d and D5e carry from a measured width to a stated gap, and it is section 4 of the paper those units walk.

What to do with a new shape. Write the decay down, hand it to diffraction_limit, divide, and check the answer at three widths. Fifteen lines of the work above is the whole recipe, and it applies to any decay a reader can write.

The one thing to carry alongside g is the formula the width was defined by. Writing the same bell the two usual ways moves the constant by exactly 1.41421356.
