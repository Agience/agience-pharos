Lesson three · the point-spread

How sharp,
how long.

The first two lessons measured a screen standing still. How much of it is real; what shape it has. Neither one ever looked at the order the rows came in.

This lesson does nothing else. It asks the question the ordered axis exists for:

How long does the record remember?

And that single question turns out to be the same one an optician asks about a lens — because “how far apart must two things be before I can tell them apart” and “how long until a record forgets” are the same arithmetic wearing different clothes.

Everything here is built from one new object: the decay. From it come a resolution limit, a shape number, a consistency check, and a set of rates.

Two records with a real ordered axis

B · the market, at full length — the same eight companies, across all 70 months. Memory is the question; sixteen rows is not enough to answer it.

A real EEG — 800 samples from four electrodes. A genuine time series, where consecutive samples really are related.

The scan sits this one out. Its rows are eight scan lines, ordered in space, and eight lags gives four points, which is too few for a decay curve. Knowing which reads a record can support is part of reading it.

Act I · The decay

Compare each row with the one τ steps later.

Take row 1 and row 2, and see how alike they are. Then row 2 and row 3. Average over the whole record. That is one number: how alike are rows one step apart.

Now do it for two steps apart. Then three. You get a list, one entry per gap, and the list is called the decay.

C(τ) = average likeness of rows τ apart

The symbol. τ is the Greek letter tau — rhymes with “now”, and it is their letter t. It is used across physics for a gap in time, which is exactly what it is counting: how many rows apart.

By construction C(0) is every row compared with itself, so it is the biggest value there is. Divide everything by it and the list starts at 1 and falls away. How fast it falls is the whole read.

Opticians have a name for this curve. Shine a point of light through a lens and it does not come out a point — it comes out a small blur. The shape of that blur is the point-spread function, and it is this curve.

Act I · The decay

The market has no memory, and that is the right answer.

The flat teal line is a finding, and one of the most robust facts in finance.

the market C(1)/C(0) = −0.053 the EEG C(1)/C(0) = +0.884

Knowing how eight companies moved last month tells you nothing about how they will move next month. If it did, somebody would already have traded on it until it stopped being true.

And lesson one said the same thing by another route. The ordered-coherence score there read z = +0.13 for the market — near zero — and +9.30 for raw prices, which is the smoothness of a price series being misread as predictability.

Two different constructions, one on the correlation between rows and one on a permutation null, agreeing that monthly returns have no memory. That agreement is worth more than either read alone.

An EEG, on the other hand, is a wave

Brain activity is continuous. A voltage measured now is physically connected to the voltage a moment ago — there is no arbitrage to compete it away.

τ 0 1 2 3 4 5 1.000 0.884 0.692 0.519 0.378 0.264

Still 26% alike five samples later. That is the shape of the blur, and everything left in this lesson is a way of putting a number on how wide it is.

A read that returns “no structure” has still measured something. The instrument did not fail on the market; it reported, correctly, that the ordered axis carries no usable memory. Confusing those two — a failed measurement and a measurement of absence — is how people end up trading on noise.

Act II · The limit

Two honest ways to say how wide the blur is.

You have a curve that starts at 1 and falls. How wide is it? There is more than one defensible answer, and the construction takes two.

1 · Add it up

Walk along the curve adding values until it first crosses zero. That total is the correlation length, written ξ.

The symbol. ξ is the Greek letter xi — usually said “zigh” or “ksee”. Physics uses it for a characteristic length, which is what it is here: how many steps the memory runs.

2 · Take its entropy

Square the curve, turn it into shares, take the entropy — the same four lines as always — and you get how many lags are really in play. Its reciprocal is the resolution:

aδ = 2−HC

The small δ hanging off the a is the Greek letter delta, their letter d, which across science means a difference or a spacing. So aδ reads as “the smallest spacing this record can resolve”.

Reading, for the EEG: HC = 4.569 bits, so 24.569 = 23.73 lags are in play, and aδ = 1 ÷ 23.73 = 0.0421.

Why aδ is called a diffraction limit. Abbe worked out in 1873 that a microscope cannot resolve two points closer together than a limit set by its aperture — no matter how much you magnify. Push past it and you get a bigger blur, not more detail.

Here the “aperture” is the record and the limit is aδ: two events closer together than this cannot be told apart, however finely the thing was sampled. The EEG can resolve to about a twenty-fourth of its length; the market to about half of its, which is another way of saying it resolves nothing.

Act II · The limit · the check

Plant a memory of known length and see if it comes back.

Everything so far is plausible. To know whether the widths are right, build records whose memory you set yourself, and see what the read returns.

These are made by a rule so simple you can say it in a sentence: each row is the previous row faded a little, plus a fresh random kick. How much it fades sets how long the memory runs, and we choose it.

planted memory: ρ = 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 steps 4000 rows, 48 channels, nothing tuned

The symbol. ρ is the Greek letter rho — said “row”. It is their letter r, and it is standing for the memory length we planted.

A read you can mark against a known answer is a read you are testing.

Act II · The limit

Multiply the two widths, and the units cancel.

The two widths disagree by a consistent factor — 1 ÷ aδ runs about 1.7× above ξ at every planted length. That is not a discrepancy; the two are measuring the same curve in different ways, and the gap between them says something about its shape.

So multiply them. One is a length and the other is a reciprocal length, so the product has no units at all:

g = ξ × aδ

Abbe’s rule has exactly this form: the resolution is a length times a constant fixed by the shape of the aperture. The famous 1.22 for a round lens is such a constant — it comes from the shape of the hole, not its size.

What it does and does not do. Measured over the same sixteenfold range: ξ moves by 10.2×, aδ by 15.2×, and g by 1.49×. So it is far steadier than either width alone — but it is not a constant, and a decay of genuinely different shape moves it.

Our two records: the EEG reads g = 0.188 and the market g = 0.485. They are not the same shape of curve — one is a smooth exponential fade, the other is a spike at zero and nothing after — and the number says so without reference to how long either one is.

Act III · The certificate

Measure the same thing a second way, on purpose.

There is a completely different route to the same length. Build a matrix out of the decay curve, take its eigenvalues, and count how many are in play — the same entropy trick, on a different object.

temporal from the curve aδ spectral from its eigenvalues aδspec their ratio ρM

For a steady, well-behaved record the two readings sit in a fixed proportion. So their ratio is a built-in consistency check — the instrument marking its own homework, with no model and nothing fitted.

And when the ratio moves, that is information. A record whose behaviour changes partway through — a regime switch, an instrument drifting, a subject falling asleep — breaks the fixed proportion, because the two routes weight the change differently.

This is the one read here that checks the others. It answers: should I believe the numbers on the last three slides?

Steady against switching

Two records, each cut into four blocks, with the ratio read on each block separately. One keeps the same memory throughout; the other changes from a memory of 2 to a memory of 16 halfway down.

steady 2.737 2.517 2.606 2.685 spread 3.2% switching 2.153 2.046 3.691 3.123 spread 24.9%

A 7.8× separation, and you can see the switch land between the second and third blocks. Neither record was labelled.

Our two: the EEG reads a ratio of 6.11, the market 1.90. Those are not comparable to each other — the check is about whether a ratio stays put across a record, not about what value it takes.

Act III · The rates

Finally: how fast does each pattern fade, and does it spin?

The decay gives one number for the whole record. But lesson one broke the record into layers, and each layer can fade at its own speed. So fit a one-step rule — given this row, what is the next one? — and read off, per layer, two things:

α how fast it fades (a rate) β how far it turns per step (a frequency)

The symbols. α is alpha, their letter a — used in lesson one for a false-alarm level and here for a decay rate, which is the same overloading problem as sigma and worth naming twice. β is beta, their letter b, and it is a per-step angle.

A big α means that pattern is gone almost immediately. A small one means it persists. And a non-zero β means the pattern rotates as it fades — oscillating at a definite frequency.

And the carrier

the market β = 0.0000 the EEG β = 3.1416

The market’s dominant pattern does not rotate at all — it just fades. The EEG’s advances by 3.1416 radians per sample, which is π — a half turn every step, so the pattern flips sign each sample.

That is the fastest rotation a sampled record can represent, and a value sitting exactly at that limit is as likely to be an artefact of the recording as a fact about the brain. Worth reporting and worth doubting — the read is right, its interpretation needs the instrument's manual.

Nine reads, one object. Every one of them came from the same decay curve or from the operator fitted to the same rows, and every one is a different question about it. None needed a threshold, a model, or a setting.

And they agree. The EEG remembers and the market does not, and it shows up nine times independently — in the curve, in both widths, in the limit, in the rates. Reads that agree from different constructions are worth more than any one of them.

The three lessons together

One — the screen. How much of this is real? A count, K, and a reconstruction from the layers that cleared the noise floor.

Two — the aperture. What shape is it? Fill fractions, an area, a capacity, a quality number, all dimensionless.

Three — the point-spread. How long does it remember? A curve, two widths, a limit, a certificate and a set of rates.

Your turn

Three problems, and the thread through all three lessons.

1 · Read a decay

τ 0 1 2 3 4 1.00 0.50 0.25 0.12 0.06

What is ξ, and does this record remember?

Answer

Add until it would cross zero — it never quite does here, so add what you have: 1.00 + 0.50 + 0.25 + 0.12 + 0.06 ≈ 1.93 (≈ is approximately equal to).
It remembers, but barely: each step keeps about half of what came before, so after four steps only 6% survives. Compare the EEG’s 4.47.

2 · Use the limit

A record of 500 rows reads aδ = 0.01. Two events happen 3 rows apart. Can the record tell them apart?

Answer

aδ is a fraction of the record’s length, so the finest resolvable gap is 0.01 × 500 = 5 rows. Two events 3 rows apart are inside the limit — they will blur into one, and no amount of re-processing separates them. You would need a finer recording, not a cleverer method.

3 · Judge a check

You cut a record into five blocks and the Mercer ratio reads 4.1, 4.0, 4.2, 7.9, 8.1. What has happened, and where?

Answer

Something changed between the third and fourth blocks. The ratio is stable at about 4.1 and then stable at about 8.0 — two steady regimes with a switch between them, not drift and not noise.

The reads either side are each fine; what you must not do is quote a single number for the whole record, because there is no single record.

The thread through all three. Every read in these lessons is built the same way: square something, turn it into shares, take the entropy, and divide by a count.

Lesson one pointed it at the column powers and got a scale. Lesson two pointed it at the layer strengths and got a fill fraction. Lesson three pointed it at the decay curve and got a resolution limit. One procedure, three objects, three completely different questions answered.

What none of it needed

No threshold. No fitted constant. No training. No setting to choose, with one exception: α, how often you are willing to be fooled, which enters at the noise floor in lesson one and nowhere else.
