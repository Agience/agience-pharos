How Many Are Working?

A record has a shape, and the shape gives one count: four sensors, eight companies, 800 rows. This unit measures a second count — how many of them are carrying the record — and the two are different numbers.

square it  ·  add down the axis  ·  divide by the total  →  a share per column

Those shares are what the second count is built from. Three operations, no settings, and nothing to choose. Here they are on the EEG's four sensors:

They are nearly equal, so all four sensors are carrying about a quarter of the record each. Hold on to that: evenly loaded is the case where the two counts agree, and the next slides make that exact.

Unit A1 counted modes standing above a floor and unit A10 asked how much record a read needs. This one asks about the record's width, and the answer is a number between 1 and the number of columns.

The count, and how to know the scale is honest

Take the shares and ask how spread out they are. The measure is entropy, in bits, and unit A5 used the same quantity on letters. The leaning symbol below is a sigma, and it means add up what follows — one term per column:

H = −∑ p log2 p   ·   the effective count = 2H

On the EEG's four shares that gives H = 1.999998 bits and an effective count of 3.999996. Which raises the only question worth asking of a new scale: how do you know it means anything?

Check it where you already know the answer. Silence one sensor and three equally loaded ones are left, so a scale that counts working columns should return exactly three:

3.000000, 2.000000, 1.000000. The scale lands on whole numbers in every case a reader can check by hand, which is what earns it the cases they cannot — like 3.999996 on the record as recorded, where the four shares differ in the fourth decimal place and the count comes in just under four.

A scale read against known values is a scale you can use. This one is calibrated by construction: give it k equal columns and it returns k.

Turn one sensor up, and the record counts for less

Take the EEG and multiply sensor 1 by a gain. Nothing is removed, nothing is added, and the other three sensors are untouched. Then read the count again:

From 4.0000 to 1.1784. At a gain of 10 the record behaves as though it had barely more than one sensor, because one sensor's share has grown until the other three's have almost vanished.

Amplifying a channel adds nothing to a record. A share is a fraction of the whole, so raising one lowers the rest — and the count follows the shares. That is the same arithmetic unit B2 uses when it changes volts to millivolts and the shares do not move: a share cannot see a change that hits every column equally, and it sees nothing else.

Which is a practical fact about instruments. A sensor with the gain wound up makes the other channels contribute less, and the effective count is where that shows.

The instrument publishes both of these. Down the columns it is H_F, and down the rows it is H_T — the same entropy of the same power share, and they agree with the arithmetic above to six decimal places on both records. So a reader can compute the count by hand or ask for it, and get one number.

  • The scale returns the count when the load is even. Three equal columns give 3.000000 and two give 2.000000, measured on this record by silencing sensors. That is what makes 4.0000 readable.
  • It never exceeds the number of columns and never falls below one. The most even case is all of them; the most concentrated is one carrying everything.
  • Gain lowers it. Turning one EEG sensor up by 10 takes the four to 1.1784. A share is a fraction, so raising one lowers the others.
  • It is four lines of arithmetic. Square, sum, normalise, take the entropy. No model, no fitting, and nothing to set.

What this count answers is how many columns the record behaves as though it had. Whether a company carrying 0.0616 of the record's power should be dropped from the study is a question about the study, and this number is one input to it.
