Entroptics / lesson A17 · a record that is two things

Did the Record Change?

Every read so far answers a question about a record taken as one thing. This unit is the case where a record is two things end to end — behaving one way, then another — and the reads have no way to say so.

The record is built, so the answer is known in advance: 800 rows, the first half with influence decaying at a rate of 0.05 and the second half at 1.50. Slow, then fast.

Six of the seven reads in this course separate that middle row from a stationary one at eight seeds, with no overlap. It looks like detection.

It is not. The changed record sits between its own two halves and close to the slow one — which is what an average looks like, not what a discovery looks like.

What the reads are really answering

Take the changed record and shuffle its rows. Every value survives; both halves are still there in the same proportion; only the order of the change is destroyed:

Interleaving takes it from 56.81 to 3.04 — down to what the fast record reads. Nothing was added or removed.

So the separation on the previous slide was real arithmetic about the wrong question. A reader comparing 56.81 against 73.82 would call the changed record slightly less resolved, when it is half one thing and half another.

Unit A11's second test asks whether a null removes what the read measures. This is the same question asked of the read: shuffle the record and see what survives, and what survived here was everything except the thing being looked for.

Split it, and compare the halves

The method costs one line: read the first half, read the second, and take the ratio between them. A record that is one thing gives halves that agree; a record that changed gives halves that do not.

Clean separation. Everything that is one thing stays under 3.48; the record that changed starts at 8.65. And the check holds on records this course has carried the whole way down — the EEG reads 1.16 and the terrain grid 1.82, both as unchanged.

This is unit C3's control turned along the ordered axis. There the two populations were land and sea and the control was a size-matched random draw; here they are early and late, and the control is the other half of the same record.

Where the line goes, and what it costs

The margin is real and it is not large. A slow record's halves genuinely differ more: one decaying at 0.05 throughout reaches 3.48 between its halves, where one at 1.50 stays under 1.14. The line has to be drawn against the slowest record a reader expects, and what this table offers is 3.48 against 8.65.

  • Whole-record reads cannot see a change. Six of seven separated a changed record from a stationary one, and shuffling the rows — same values, order destroyed — moved the reading from 56.81 to 3.04. They were reading the average.
  • Splitting can. One line, and the ratio between the halves separates every case on the table.
  • It works on real records too. The EEG at 1.16 and the terrain grid at 1.82 both read as one thing, which is what they are.
  • Say which line you drew. 3.48 is the widest an unchanged record reached here, across five kinds and eight seeds. A reader with slower records than these needs their own number.

What this unit establishes is that a change in a record's structure is invisible to a read taken over the whole of it, and visible to the same read taken over halves. Two questions remain for a reader working on their own records: where to split one you did not build, and what to do when the change falls away from the midpoint.
