Entroptics / a grade 10 lesson

A lesson in reading data

The screen,
the lens,
the projection.

Every measurement you will ever meet arrives as a grid of numbers — a photo, a brain scan, a share price, a radio telescope pointed at a galaxy.

Entroptics reads that grid the way an optician reads a lens: it asks the data itself how sharply it can be seen, throws away what is only blur, and keeps what is really there.

We follow two real grids the whole way down — the same two, at every step — so each new idea can be checked against something you already understand. Both are small enough to print in full.

Everything is done with maths you already have: squares, fractions, logs, square roots, averages and ratios. Every number was computed from the real data with the real software.

the scan

One handwritten 0, read by a scanner in 1998. 8 rows × 8 columns of whole numbers, 0 to 16.

the market

Eight companies, seventeen months of closing prices in dollars — 17 rows × 8 columns, ending September 2020. Step 0 turns it into sixteen rows of change.

Step 0, then seven

0 choose what a cell holds 1 square it → power 2 divide by the total → shares 3 entropy → how many 4 divide → the scale 5 whiten and fold → the screen 6 split into layers → the floor 7 add the survivors → the answer

Act I · Organise

Here they both are, exactly as they arrive.

the scan — 8 × 8

A scanner read 43 people’s handwriting in 1998 and reduced each digit to 64 numbers — 0 for white, 16 for black. Read the numbers: the big values trace a loop.

Each cell already holds a measurement: how much ink landed at that spot. Nothing needs doing to it.

the market — 17 × 8

Seventeen months of closing prices for eight technology companies (Yahoo Finance), in dollars. Darker means bigger.

Each cell holds a level — what one share cost that month. And you can see the trouble already: GOOGL’s column is dark all the way down and XRX’s is pale all the way down, 1629.5 against 13.9, a factor of 117.

Both are matrices — rectangles of numbers — and that is the only kind of object in this lesson. But only one of them is ready to be read, and the next slide is the difference.

Act I · Organise · Step 0

Deciding what goes in a cell. This part is yours.

A share price is a fact about a company’s whole history and how many shares it ever issued. It is not a measurement of anything that happened this month. Hand the price grid over as it stands and two things go wrong, both of which you can measure.

1 · A price column is smooth by construction

Today’s price is nearly yesterday’s — that is what a price is. Ask the instrument how much order it finds down the rows, both ways:

prices z = +9.30 ← “hugely ordered” changes z = +0.13 ← about zero

Near zero is the right answer — next month’s move is famously hard to predict. The +9.30 is a price series being smooth, misread as a market being predictable.

2 · The columns are not comparable

Dollars of Google are not dollars of Xerox in any useful sense. Count how many of the eight channels are really in play (Act II does this properly) and the price grid answers 1.5 of 8 — a true statement about the numbers and a false one about the market. It has read the share counts, not the companies.

One subtraction fixes both

Take each row away from the one before it, and divide by where you started:

IBM May 103.5 → Jun 113.7 (113.7 − 103.5) ÷ 103.5 = +9.9% GOOGL May 1106.5 → Jun 1082.8 (1082.8 − 1106.5) ÷ 1106.5 = −2.1%

Seventeen rows of prices become sixteen rows of change — you lose one, because the first month has nothing before it.

Both problems go at once. A change is not smooth, so the spurious order disappears. And a per cent is a per cent whether the share costs $14 or $1600, so the eight columns are finally the same kind of number.

And the instrument cannot do this for you. Handed a column of numbers it has no way to know whether they are levels or measurements — they look identical. Choosing what a cell holds is the one part of this that is entirely a human judgement, and it happens before step 1.

Two different things, easily confused

Converting to a common unit — dollars to per cent — is what we just did, and it is required. Dividing each column by its own measured spread is a different operation, the instrument does it for you at step 5, and doing it yourself first breaks something. Act II shows exactly what.

Act I · Organise

The working grid, and the two axes.

the market — 16 × 8, monthly change

Amber is up, teal is down. Two rows are outlined and you can see why without being told: in February and March 2020 everything went the same way at once. This is the grid every remaining step uses.

Now decide what the two directions mean. The market makes it obvious:

  • Rows are ordered. March 2020 comes after February 2020. Swap them and you have destroyed the crash — the order it happened in was the story.
  • Columns are channels. IBM sits beside AAPL because of how somebody saved the file. Put ADBE first instead and nothing whatever about the market has changed.

The scan is the same shape of object: its rows are the scanner’s eight passes, top to bottom, and its columns are positions across. You cannot shuffle those rows either.

One honest difference. The scan’s rows are ordered in space, not time — nothing is happening, it is a picture read top to bottom. Everything below works identically for both, but where the lesson says “you must never average two moments”, it is the market it has in mind.

So: columns may be merged. Rows never are.

Act I · Organise · Step 1

Square every number. Now it is power.

The first thing we do to either grid is square every cell.

P = W2 (each cell, one at a time)

Two reasons, both of them physics you already know:

  • Squaring kills the sign. A month where a share fell 9% moved as much as one where it rose 9%. Down is the opposite of up, and the same size as up. Squaring says exactly that.
  • Squaring is what energy is. Brightness goes as amplitude squared, kinetic energy as speed squared, electrical power as voltage squared. Same rule everywhere.

Then add up each column: how much total movement came down that channel across the whole record.

Act I · Organise · Step 2

Turn the totals into shares that add up to 1.

Divide each column total by the grand total. Every column now holds a share of the movement, and the shares must add to exactly 1 — a free check on your arithmetic.

the scan 980.0 ÷ 3070.0 = 0.3192 the market 3165.3 ÷ 11979.9 = 0.2642

Here is what that buys. A moment ago one grid was measured in ink and the other in per cent. Now both are just eight numbers that add to 1, and every remaining step treats them identically.

The units are gone, on purpose. Whether the scanner reported 0–16 or 0–255, whether the market is in dollars or yen, the shares come out the same. This is why one piece of software can read a handwriting scan and a radio telescope without being told which is which.

A list of non-negative numbers that adds to 1 is a probability distribution. That is the door into step 3.

Act II · Scale · Step 3

Entropy: how many are really in play?

“It has 8 columns” is a fact about the file. “It is really using about 5 of them” is a fact about the signal. Shannon’s entropy turns the second sentence into a number, and it works on any list of shares — which is exactly what step 2 handed us.

H = − Σ p × log2 p then effective count = 2H

First, that symbol

Σ is a Greek capital S. You say it “sigma”, and the S stands for sum. It is an instruction:

“Work out whatever comes after me — once for every item in the list — then add all the answers together.”

So Σ p × log2 p is not one calculation. It is eight calculations, one per column, added up at the end. And p is the list of shares step 2 handed us, one for each column.

Both p’s in a term are the same number

That is the part worth pausing on. Inside one calculation, the p on the left and the p inside the log are the same single share — you use it twice.

Reminder: log2 x asks “2 to what power gives x?” So log2 8 = 3 and log2(1/8) = −3. Shares are below 1, so their logs are negative — which is exactly why the formula opens with a minus sign.

The whole sum, written out — A · the scan

Step 2 gave us this list of eight shares:

p = 0.0000 0.0228 0.3192 0.1928 0.1244 0.2541 0.0866 0.0000

Take the third one. Use it twice in the same calculation:

− 0.3192 × log2(0.3192) = − 0.3192 × (−1.647) = 0.5259

Now do that eight times — once per column — and add. That is what the Σ was asking for:

0.0000 ← empty column, contributes nothing + 0.1244 + 0.5259 ← the one worked out above + 0.4579 + 0.3741 + 0.5022 + 0.3057 + 0.0000 ← the other empty column ──────── 2.2902 bits 22.2902 = 4.89

Add that column yourself and it comes to 2.2902. An empty column contributes exactly nothing, because we take 0 × log 0 = 0 — so 2H counts only columns that carry something.

Two extremes worth knowing

all eight shares equal at 1/8 every term is −(1/8)×(−3) = 0.375 eight of them: 3 bits 2³ = 8 one column holds everything, p = 1 its term is −1 × log2(1) = −1×0 = 0 all the rest are 0 too: 0 bits 2⁰ = 1

So H runs from 0 (everything in one column) up to 3 (spread evenly over all eight), and 2H from 1 to 8. The scan’s 4.89 sits between them, which is the whole point of measuring it.

Act II · Scale · Step 4

Divide by the width, and the number starts to travel.

2H is a count: 4.89 columns of the scan, 7.10 of the market. Useful — and useless the moment you want to compare two records that are not the same width. A count of 148 on a 256-wide image and a count of 4.89 on an 8-wide scan cannot be put beside each other at all.

So divide it by the width you had:

2H ÷ F the fraction of the axis in use

Two new letters, so: what they are called and what they stand for.

δ is the Greek letter delta — their letter d, and in every branch of science it means a difference or a spacing. Here it is the spacing between cells you can actually tell apart, and it is never below 1. The fraction above is simply 1 ÷ δ, the same fact upside down.

the scan 4.89 ÷ 8 = 0.61 → δ = 1.64 the market 7.10 ÷ 8 = 0.89 → δ = 1.13

Now it is a fraction of the axis actually in use, it has no units, it cannot exceed 1, and it means the same thing on a grid of any size. That is what the entropy was for.

Read it as a zoom level. 0.89 says the market is using nearly all the room it was given — it is not zoomed in on anything. 0.61 says the scan is spending about two fifths of its width on nothing.

The library reports the reciprocal, δ = F ÷ 2H — the scan’s 1.64 says it spends 1.64 columns per column’s worth. Fraction or spacing, it is one measurement. Act III has a different fill fraction, of a different object; this one is the axis.

Act II · Scale · The fold

The same number, used a second way: how far you may squash.

δ also answers a practical question. If the scan is using 4.89 columns’ worth, why store 8? Replace neighbouring columns with their weighted average — exactly how your phone resizes a photo — and keep round(F / δ) of them.

That is the fold, and it is only honest if neighbouring columns are actually related. Two separate facts are needed:

  • Concentration — how far a fold could go. That is δ, which we have.
  • Continuity — whether folding is allowed at all. Is column 3 next to column 4 in any real sense?

Entropy cannot tell you the second, because shuffling the columns does not change H by one bit. So a second test runs: are neighbouring columns more alike than a random reshuffle would make them? The score must beat 1.645 — the level a reshuffle passes only 5% of the time.

The market fails it in the most obvious way possible. IBM, AAPL, MSFT, XRX — that order came from a spreadsheet. Averaging MSFT with XRX because they are adjacent is not a summary of anything. It scores −1.12: neighbours are, if anything, slightly less alike than strangers.

The scan should pass — pixel 3 really is beside pixel 4 — and scores 0.73, the right sign and nowhere near enough. With 8 rows of evidence there is no proving it. So both keep all 8 columns.

And be clear what the fold is for: cost, not accuracy. Measured on a planted signal, rebuilding from a folded screen is never more accurate than rebuilding from the full one — it converges on the same answer as the signal gets smoother. What it buys is a smaller screen and a cheaper decomposition, for a penalty measured at about zero. The fraction in use is the read; the fold is free compression that it licenses.

Act II · Scale · Step 5

Whiten — and a third case where the read returns nothing.

One step still has to happen, and it comes after the fill fraction was read, not before. That ordering matters, and the next slide is why. Two things happen to each channel:

  1. Subtract its middle. Each column’s own median, so every channel is centred on zero.
  2. Divide by its typical wobble — the median distance from that middle, not the standard deviation, because one enormous month drags a standard deviation around and XRX has exactly such a month.

Here are the eight companies’ wobbles. They are not the same:

IBM AAPL MSFT XRX AMZN DELL GOOG ADBE 10.30 9.34 5.93 9.56 8.15 11.49 6.75 9.49

DELL wobbles about twice as much as MSFT. So divide each by its own and be done? The read returns nothing — and for the third time in this lesson the reason is evidence.

A wobble measured from only 16 rows is itself a noisy number. So it asks: is the spread across these eight bigger than what sixteen rows of pure luck would produce anyway?

spread actually seen 0.0429 what 16 rows give free 0.0850 ← bigger

It is not. The eight wobbles sit inside the noise of measuring a wobble at all. So all eight are pooled and divided by one number, 9.4145.

This one does change the answer

Measured against a planted truth: when the channels are genuinely equal, whitening costs a little (RMSE 0.85×, slightly worse). When one channel is a hundred times louder it is 8.88× better, because without it the loudest channel simply wins the decomposition and layer 1 becomes that channel. It is insurance with a small premium.

Act II · Scale · The order

Why the scale is read before the whitening — and what that asks of you.

It looks backwards. Step 3 counts how many channels are in play using raw numbers that might be in wildly different units — surely you make them comparable first, then count?

No. And it is worth seeing why, because the two steps want opposite things.

Take a case where folding is obviously right: a narrow bright line, four channels wide, on a 128-channel axis of noise. Fold that to about a dozen cells and nothing is lost.

the line holds 40.2% of the power → 2ᴴ = 12.6 of 128, fold to 13 ✓ now whiten first, then count: the line holds 2.1% of the power → 2ᴴ = 127.5 of 128, fold to 128 ✗

Whitening divides every channel by its own wobble, so the 124 channels holding nothing but noise get amplified until they are exactly as loud as the four holding the line. Afterwards every channel looks equally busy, and the concentration the fold exists to find has been erased.

The two steps are opposites, deliberately. Step 3 asks where is the power — it needs raw amplitudes. Step 5 asks are the channels on a common footing — its whole job is to flatten the very differences step 3 just measured. Run them the other way round and the first has nothing left to find.

But it leaves a condition on you. Because step 3 reads raw amplitudes, it only means something when the channels are already in the same units. Eight companies in per-cent, or 16 384 radio channels in the same counts — fine. Eight share prices in dollars, where Google’s number has four digits and Xerox’s has two — not fine: the read comes back as 1.5 of 8.

Nothing in the grid can tell you which case you are in. Multiplying a column by ten looks exactly like that channel being ten times louder. So it is a fact about your data you have to bring with you — which is what step 0 was for.

But note which operation step 0 performed. It converted every cell to a common unit — dollars to per cent, each cell divided by its own starting price. It did not divide each column by that column’s measured spread. That second thing is what step 5 does, and doing it first is the order measured above. Convert the units; leave the spread alone.

Act III · Project · Step 6

A layer is a multiplication table.

Take a column of numbers and a row of numbers and fill in the times table between them. Cell (i, j) is just row-valuei × column-valuej.

Something remarkable is true of every table built this way: every row is a multiple of the same row, and every column a multiple of the same column. The whole 4×3 table — 12 numbers — is really 7 numbers in disguise.

That is what a layer is: one shape down the rows multiplied by one shape across the columns. One thing happening; a pattern in time, and a pattern across the channels.

Now the big fact. Any grid at all can be written as a stack of layers added together, and there is exactly one way to do it that puts them in order of strength.

S = s1·layer1 + s2·layer2 + s3·layer3 + … s1 ≥ s2 ≥ s3 ≥ … ≥ 0

≥ is greater than or equal to — the ordinary > with a line under it, allowing ties. So the strengths never increase as you go down the stack, though two may be equal.

Where those layers come from is the next two slides, and it turns out to be multiply-and-add repeated. Nothing you cannot do on paper.

Building one layer

× 2 1 3  ┌────────────── 1 │ 2 1 3 2 │ 4 2 6 3 │ 6 3 9 4 │ 8 4 12

Row 3 is (6, 3, 9), which is 3× the pattern (2, 1, 3). Every row is. The (1,2,3,4) says when; the (2,1,3) says where.

Why this is the right question to ask

A market crash is one thing that happens: a moment (a shape down the rows) hitting every company at once (a shape across the columns). It is a layer. Noise is not — noise has no single shape, so it has to spread itself thinly over many weak layers. That difference is the entire method.

Act III · Project · Step 6

Where each layer comes from: best fit, subtract, repeat.

The layers are not handed to you. You find them one at a time, by the most obvious method there is.

  1. Of every multiplication table you could possibly write down, find the one that comes closest to your grid. That is layer 1.
  2. Subtract it. What is left is a new grid — everything layer 1 could not explain.
  3. Do the whole thing again on what is left. That is layer 2. Then layer 3. Keep going until nothing is left.

This is also why they come out in order. Nobody sorts them. Each round takes the biggest thing still standing, so the next can only be smaller. s1 ≥ s2 ≥ s3 is a consequence of the method, not a step in it.

Peeling greedily usually gives a worse answer than solving the whole problem at once. Here it does not: the greedy peel provably lands on the exact same layers as the full decomposition. That is a real theorem, and the numbers on the right are it happening.

Act III · Project · Step 6

Finding the best table: push a guess through, and back.

“Find the closest multiplication table” sounded like the hard part. It is not. A table needs a row pattern and a column pattern, so:

  1. Guess a column pattern v. Any guess. A bad one is fine.
  2. Push it through: u = M v — “if the columns look like that, what do the rows look like?”
  3. Push it back: v = M′ u — the same question in reverse.
  4. Scale each answer to length 1 so it cannot run away, and go round again.

Both moves are ordinary multiply-and-add. Nothing else appears.

Why it lands on the strongest one. Every pattern hidden in the grid is amplified on each round trip, but not equally — each grows by its own strength. Below, the top pattern grows 21.79× per round and the runner-up only 2.89×. After three rounds the leader is ahead by (21.79 ÷ 2.89)3 ≈ 430 and everything else has effectively vanished.

A symbol in passing: ≈ is approximately equal to — an equals sign that has been shaken. It marks a number close enough for the purpose but not exact.

So you never search for the best table. You start anywhere and let the grid amplify its own loudest pattern until that is all you can see.

Three rounds of multiply-and-add from a guess chosen to be unhelpful, and it has stopped moving.

What this is called

Everything in these two slides has a name: it is the singular value decomposition, the SVD. You will meet it again in linear algebra, in statistics as principal component analysis, and in every image-compression course there is. The dictionary is exact:

our word the textbook’s word ——————————————————————————————————————— a layer a rank-1 component its strength s a singular value its row pattern u a left singular vector its column pattern v a right singular vector the whole stack S = U Σ V′

The strengths obey Pythagoras

every cell squared: 3²+15²+4²+12²+5²+8² = 483 the two strengths squared: 21.7866² + 2.8888² = 483

Layer 2’s patterns come out at right angles to layer 1’s — layer 2 contains none of layer 1 — so the strengths add like the sides of a right triangle. That is why “layer 1 carries half the power” is a sentence you are allowed to say, and why a floor can compare s² against a noise level at all.

Act III · Project · Step 6

How strong is the strongest layer of pure noise?

We have a stack of layers for each grid. Now the question that makes the whole thing work: fill a grid with nothing but random numbers — no signal at all — and take its layers. How big is the biggest?

Not zero. With a hundred random numbers, some of them will line up into a pattern by pure luck. The question is how much luck to expect, and that has an exact answer:

biggest noise layer ≈ σ × ( √N + √F )

Careful: that is the same letter as before, in its small form, and it means something completely different.

Σ — big sigma — was an instruction: add these up.
σ — small sigma — is a quantity: the typical size of one noisy cell. It is the standard name for “how much this wobbles” throughout statistics, which is why it is worth keeping even though the letter is doing double duty.

√ is the square root — √9 = 3, the number that gives 9 when multiplied by itself. N is the rows and F the live columns, so after Act II we have all three for both grids.

Why square roots?

Because random things cancel. Add 100 random numbers that are equally likely to be plus or minus and you do not get 100 — you get about √100 = 10. It is the same rule that says a coin flipped 100 times lands about 50±5 heads — ± is plus or minus, so anywhere from 45 to 55 — not 50±50. Luck grows, but it grows like a square root.

So the bar rises with the grid. A bigger grid gives luck more chances, so it needs a higher bar. That is why you cannot pick one threshold and use it everywhere — and why nobody has to, because the shape of the grid already says where the bar goes.

The one thing you choose. The formula marks the middle of where noise lands; half the time luck does a bit better. So a small extra allowance is added, set by one number: α. That is alpha, the Greek letter a and the first of their alphabet; statistics uses it for exactly this quantity — how often you are willing to be fooled. At α = 5%, one pure-noise grid in twenty will fool this test. That number cannot be measured — it is a judgement about whether a false alarm or a missed crash costs you more. Everything else here is arithmetic.

One layer each. On the market that is the correct and well-known answer: there is a market, and after you have accounted for it what remains is eight companies doing their own thing. On the scan it is a statement about how little evidence 64 numbers contain — one layer is all you can claim from a grid that small.

No threshold was chosen anywhere. 5.65 and 6.31 came out of the two grids’ own shapes and their own middle rows. Hand the same code a 16 384-channel radio telescope and it computes a third number by the identical arithmetic, without being told anything.

Where do 4.6880 and 6.8922 come from?

Each is F × cF × (N−1)/N — two small corrections that turn a median into a variance. The median of a total sits a little below its average, by a known amount cF = (1 − 2/9F)3, and (N−1)/N accounts for having centred each channel first. For the scan: 6 × 0.89296 × 7/8 = 4.6880. For the market: 8 × 0.91897 × 15/16 = 6.8922. Both are derived, neither is tuned. The middle factor is shown to five places and the product uses its full value, so the market line lands a digit below what the printed factor alone gives.

Act III · Project · Step 7

The projection: add back only the layers that made it.

One layer cleared the floor on the market. So rebuild the whole 16×8 grid out of that one layer, and leave out the other seven.

Ŝ = s1L1 (and stop)

The little roof on Ŝ is a hat, said “S-hat”. It is the standard mark for an estimate of something: S is the screen we measured, Ŝ is our reconstruction of it.

That is the whole of step 7. It is addition, and on this grid it is one term.

You subtract. You never add. That layer was read out of the measurement itself — nothing was drawn in, no template matched, no shape assumed. If the crash were not in the numbers it could not appear in Ŝ.

This is why it is called a projection: the same word as a shadow on a wall. A shadow can lose information about the object. It cannot invent any.

And on this grid it has a plain-English reading. Ŝ is what each month would have looked like if only the market had moved. Whatever is left over is what each company did on its own.

Do it twice and nothing changes the second time — the layer you kept is the only layer there. That property has a name, idempotence, and it is what tells you this is a projection: applying it twice gives what applying it once gave.

Act III · Project · The check

But is any of it right? Mark it against a known answer.

Everything so far has been persuasive. The market really does look like it has a market in it — but we would think that either way. To check a detector you need a case where the answer is written down in advance.

So here is one built on purpose, and we will say exactly what went into it:

256 rows (time) × 128 channels one brief flash, drifting across the band Gaussian noise, σ = 1.0 exactly plus a narrowband tone on channels 41-42 83% of the rows contain no flash at all

Same seven steps, nothing adjusted. The instrument is told none of the above.

middle row’s power = 125.7397 divide by 126.8371 σ² = 0.9913 σ = 0.9957 √255 = 15.9687 √128 = 11.3137 floor ≈ 0.9957 × 27.2824 = 27.16 exact: 27.42 K = 3

We planted 1.0000. It read back 0.9957 — wrong by four parts in a thousand, from a record that is five-sixths noise, with nobody telling it which rows were which. And K = 3: the flash, its drift, and the tone.

Scored against the truth

agreement with what we planted before: 0.2989 after: 0.7961 the tone on channel 41 before: 2.15 after: 0.13

We can score this one because we know what went in. On the market we cannot — which is exactly why a case like this gets checked first.

Act III · Project · What the floor assumes

The assumption the floor makes, and a record that violates it.

Three times now, the instrument has read σ off a record it knew nothing about. How? By assuming one thing:

that most of the record is not signal.

That is why it takes the middle row. In the planted case 83% of the rows held nothing but noise, so the middle row was noise and σ came out right. The market is the same shape of thing: most months are ordinary, so the middle month is ordinary.

Now take the MRI from Act II and watch it fail. Measure that image and you find:

56.7% of the cells are exactly 0 smallest value that is not 0: 256 nothing at all in between

The background is a hard mask — somebody thresholded that scan and set the outside to zero before saving it. There is no noise in the file at all. And a typical column of it is 77% anatomy, so the middle row is not noise either.

The floor returned a number anyway, because the formula always returns a number. It just measured the spread of the head.

Which is why it is a slot, not a rule

The real library does not hard-code the assumption. The floor is something you can replace: hand it a signal-free window, a scrambled copy of your own data, or a noise model from your instrument, and it uses that instead. The built-in one is a good guess, and a guess is all it can be until somebody tells it what quiet looks like.

What you actually used

  • Squares — to turn a measurement into power
  • Fractions that sum to 1 — to make it unit-free
  • log2 and 2x — to count how many channels are really in play
  • Division — width over effective width, giving the scale δ
  • Weighted averages — to fold, when folding is allowed
  • Multiplication tables — the layers
  • Square roots — where the noise floor sits

Not one thing on that list is past Year 10.

The idea worth keeping. Nearly every number in a data pipeline is normally a guess somebody typed: how many pixels, how many results, what counts as a detection. Each one is a place the pipeline can quietly go wrong when the data changes.

Every one of them here was computed from the data instead. That is the whole argument, and the arithmetic that carries it is the arithmetic you already have.

Your turn

Four problems. Everything you need is on the slides.

1 · Find the scale

W = 2 0 2 0 2 0 2 0

Square it, total each column, turn the totals into shares, find H, then 2H, then δ. How wide should this grid be folded to?

Answer

Powers per column: 8, 0, 8, 0. Total 16. Shares: 0.5, 0, 0.5, 0.
H = −(0.5×(−1) + 0.5×(−1)) = 1 bit, so 2H = 2.
δ = 4 ÷ 2 = 2 — twice as wide as it needs to be. Fold to 2 columns — if, and only if, the columns pass the continuity test too.

2 · Find the floor

A screen has N = 100 rows and F = 25 live columns, and its typical noisy cell is σ = 2. How strong is the strongest layer pure noise could produce?

Answer

√99 = 9.95, √25 = 5, sum 14.95. Times σ = 2 gives about 29.9.
So a layer at 40 is signal; a layer at 22 is not distinguishable from luck.

3 · Compare two records

record P: F = 32 2H = 12.8 record Q: F = 512 2H = 128.0

Which record is using more of the room it was given? The counts say Q by a mile. Work out the fraction in use for each and answer properly.

Answer

P: 12.8 ÷ 32 = 0.40  ·  Q: 128 ÷ 512 = 0.25.
P is, by a good margin. Q has ten times the count and uses a smaller fraction of its axis — which is exactly why the count cannot be compared across widths and the fraction can.

4 · Say why

Somebody proposes shrinking the market grid from 16×8 to 8×8 by averaging each pair of months together, arguing that we were happy enough to discuss folding the companies. What have they destroyed?

Answer

February and March 2020 would be averaged into one row. The crash and its worst month become a single middling number, and layer 1 — the thing that found them — has nothing left to find.

Companies are channels: relabel them and nothing breaks, so merging two neighbours is at worst a fair summary of two related things. Months are ordered. The gap between one row and the next is the measurement: how fast something changed, how long it lasted, what came before what. Average two rows and every one of those answers is now wrong, and nothing in the data will tell you it happened.

This is why δ for the row axis is fixed at 1 in the real software. That fixed 1 is written into the software.
