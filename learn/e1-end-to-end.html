Unit E1 · the capstone

One Question,
All the Way Down

Every unit before this one taught a piece. This one takes a single sentence and puts it through them in order — text, to coordinates, to a screen, to a read — and reports what happened.

Chlorophyll in the leaf absorbs sunlight, and the plant converts water and carbon dioxide into glucose and oxygen.

Three steps, each measured: what the store held, where the chain stopped, and what the reads do on a record with no ordered axis. Every number on these slides is the one the chain produced.

Step one: what the store actually knew

The frame came back 18 × 512 — one row per token, as promised. Then the count that changes the reading of everything after it:

18tokens in the sentence
3the store could place

chlorophyll in the leaf absorbs sunlight and plant converts water carbon dioxide into glucose oxygen

Some of those are function words with no hypernym tree and are correctly absent — unit C1 taught that this construction reads nouns only. But chlorophyll, leaf, oxygen and water are ordinary nouns.

So read “out of vocabulary” precisely. It does not mean “not a word”. It means this store's corpus does not carry that concept — and on this node it does not. The ontology is a store, not a dictionary, and what a word means here is a measurement against a particular corpus that a particular host pointed at.

Which is also why the previous slide matters more than it first appears. The store decides the vocabulary; the import decides the store.

Is that about the sentence, or about the store?

The previous slide reports 15 of 18 tokens unknown, and says that is a statement about this store's corpus. Read that carefully: the number is a fact about one sentence, and the sentence beside it is a claim about a corpus. Eighteen tokens cannot carry it.

So: five more ordinary English sentences, deliberately on different subjects, through the same entry point in the same process.

71 of 80 tokens — 88.8% — are out of vocabulary, and the first sentence was the easiest of the six. The share runs from 0.833 to 1.000, and at the top of that range every token in the sentence is unknown. This is not a sentence that happened to be unlucky.

Which is what the claim needed to be worth making. It now says something about the store: on ordinary English prose about committees, coffee, rain, engines and football, this corpus resolves about one token in nine. The chain downstream is therefore not carrying a degraded signal, it is carrying almost none — and that is the thing the aperture goes on to read as zero.

Step two: where the chain actually stopped

With most tokens unplaceable, the call emitted rows anyway. It emitted a muted fallback row per unknown token — by a mechanism whose own docstring calls it deprecated, and which is still the default because changing it *“changes retrieval behaviour and the A/B has not run”*.

So the chain continued, carrying a frame with almost nothing in it. The next stage is an aperture read, and it is the first thing in the pipeline able to tell:

K_signal  =  0

Nothing resolved above the floor. The chain did read zero — one stage later than the information was lost.

That gap is the lesson, and it generalises past this system. A pipeline reads zero where something can measure, and the information can go missing earlier, and those are different places. The tokeniser knew perfectly well it had failed on fifteen tokens; it had no instrument to say so with, so it emitted its best surface guess. The instrument two stages down had no idea a substitution had occurred — it simply read what arrived and reported nothing there.

The exact zero is what saves it. Because the read lands on exactly zero and returns no small number, the failure surfaces as a plain zero in place of a weak result a reader could have acted on.

Step three: the control, on a record with no order

To show the chain works when the corpus does have the words, the coordinates are built directly from the ontology — unit C1's method, which is known good — and twelve nouns from one topic are read against twelve unrelated ones. If the reads mean anything, those two should not look alike.

The result is structural. Every read in Track A is about structure along an ordered axis — how a signal decays from one step to the next, whether the order carries anything, what happens across a boundary. A bag of words has no such axis. Twelve rows in an arbitrary order are twelve independent points, and there is genuinely nothing there to resolve.

Which is exactly why the real entry point emits “one ordered row per token”, and why unit A5 — reading an axis made of symbols — is a unit at all. The order is not packaging. The order is the thing being measured.

What the trace showed

One question, three steps, each step measured where it stood.

stepwhat happened
the store's vocabulary15 of 18 tokens absent from this corpus
the unknown tokensemitted fallback rows by a deprecated mechanism
the aperture readK_signal = 0 — zero, one stage downstream of the loss
the positive controlresolved 0 of 9 readings, at three channel widths

Two things the whole method rests on are visible in this one trace. A claim is worth what its control is worth: this control resolved nothing on an unordered bag, which marks the edge of what these reads can be asked — they answer questions about structure along an axis, and a record with no such axis sits outside their scope. And an exact zero is loud where a weak answer is quiet: the chain lost its information at one step, and reported an exact zero at the next step, which is where a reader can see it.

Everything on these slides is recomputed on every build, so the counts track the store itself.

Unit E2 is the companion to this one: it runs the same instrument over a record whose answer was withheld until after the reading, which is the only version of this trace that cannot be tuned after the fact.
