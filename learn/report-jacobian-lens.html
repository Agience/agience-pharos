Reading a Jacobian lens as an optical lens

Six language models, four families, widths 512–2560. What a corpus-averaged transport carries, how much of a residual stream it spans, and where two models can be made to meet.

What was asked

The Jacobian lens transports a residual-stream vector into the final-layer basis and decodes it with the model’s unembedding. On that readout Anthropic identify a global workspace holding “on the order of tens” of concepts and occupying 6–10% of activation variance — both obtained through a sparse decomposition and a chosen activity threshold.

Entroptics reads a signal as a finite optical aperture whose resolution its own entropy fixes. Everything below is measured from published lens files and residual streams, with no fitted constant and every read scored against a null.

A residual stream adds h to everything downstream, so the transport tends to the identity with depth. Removing it is an orthogonal projection onto span(I) — exact, rather than a fit — and it is a precondition for every other read: without it the depth trend inverts entirely.

Result

The transport covers 14% → 38% of the stream’s resolved subspace, rising with depth, at 17–73× the null. A random rank-K matrix sits at 0.9–1.1× chance across every layer, so the separation is a property of this transport rather than of the geometry — which is the check the retracted complement read failed.

Read on M, not on J. The transport acquires an identity core with depth (J → αI), and an identity covers everything trivially: on raw J this curve climbs to 72% at the last layer, measuring identity dominance rather than what the readout carries. Removing it is an orthogonal projection, not a fit.

The étendue ceiling

Two models of different size, meeting

A screen lets the two sides keep their own native bases. Each side has its own conversion into a shared one, and the only requirement is that the conversion be the system’s own rather than a fitted alignment. That is what lets models of different width interoperate at all.

Qwen3.5-0.8B and Qwen3.5-4B are 1024 and 2560 wide. Each enters through its own Jacobian and its own readout, which lands both in the token basis their tokenizers already share — all 248,077 ids identical. Nothing is trained to make this work: the conversions are the ones the models already have. Restricting to 4096 tokens carrying 94.4% of both sides’ output mass is the scaling lever, taking the shared basis down by 60× while keeping almost all the signal.

How far the linearisation actually carries

Two corrections are needed before the transport can be scored against the model’s own final residual. It is a first-order expansion, so its prediction is affine and carries an offset. And its target is the pre-norm residual — hidden_states[-1] arrives with the final norm already applied, at mean per-token norm 156.1 against 52.1 for its neighbour, while J maps into the un-normalised stream. RMSNorm is invertible up to per-token scale, so the right target is recoverable exactly as a direction, and direction is what fixes the logits anyway.

The curve is monotone and low for most of the network: 0.006 at layer 0, 0.047 at layer 8, 0.111 at layer 16, crossing half only at relative depth 0.90 and reaching 0.852 at layer 30. Mean cos2 over the first quarter of depth is 0.020. At the depths a lens is most wanted for — early and middle, where the logit lens is known to fail — a shared mean-Jacobian transport reproduces almost none of the token-to-token variation in the final residual.

The floor holds across every scoring; the ceiling moves with it. Three scorings were tried — an uncentred affine R2 against the post-norm frame, the raw product against the recovered pre-norm direction, and the readout-space form above. Through layer 16 they agree to within 0.003. At layer 30 they read 0.788, 0.691 and 0.852 — a spread of 0.16 driven entirely by scoring choice. The low reach at early and middle depth survives every metric by two orders of magnitude and is quoted as a number; the saturation point moves with the choice and is quoted as a range.

What fixes its scope. It measures the shared Jacobian as one linear map across tokens, while each J averages a per-token local linearisation; the two differ by exactly the variation measured here. So a low cos2 establishes one thing: the transport reproduces little of the final state's token-varying part.

The same measurement orders the catalogue’s two Qwen3.5‑4B fits against expectation: the n = 417 fit is the better linearisation on 24 of 31 layers, unanimous across all six streams on every layer from 6 upward. More fit data produced a worse linearisation here.

Three explanations excluded, and the scope that remains

Storage is identical — both files are 0.41 GB at the same shape and dtype, differing only in the recorded n_prompts. The evaluation sequences come from wikitext‑2 test while a fit is conventionally taken on train, which matters: a prompt inside both fit sets carries 1/417 of the smaller mean against 1/1000 of the larger, and would hand the smaller fit a 2.4× advantage on it by construction. And the ordering survives both targets and both statistics.

What remains is scope. One model, one corpus family, and the only fit pair the catalogue publishes — a property of these two artefacts on held-out wikitext.

How the measurement is checked

Every number above is scored against a null. Four checks establish that the reads fire on structure and not on chance, and the fourth states the size of the difference the read can resolve.

The read has a stated resolution, and the cheap alternatives stop short of it

A ranking check that compares one pair of maps returns a verdict but no scale. This one degrades a known transport by a tunable amount and measures the resulting gap in held-out transport error, so the question becomes the one an instrument can answer: how large must a difference be before the read resolves it?

Coverage is monotone in that gap and orders every layer correctly at 0.07% under lost rank (p = 0.03) and 1.04% under added noise (p = 0.008), decaying to chance below. The gap is in the corrected metric above — centred cosine to the pre-norm residual direction. Scored against the uncentred error on the post-norm frame instead, the noise figure comes out three times smaller. Two cheaper reads of the same frame stop short of this. Participation ratio never separates significantly, reaching only 0.88 (p = 0.07) at a 7.5% gap. Norm ratio scores 0.00 on every noise row and 1.00 on every rank row — so it reports which degradation was applied rather than whether quality fell, so reading it as a quality score requires already knowing the answer. Coverage points the same way for both modes, and that is the argument for it.

Stated scope

The calibration covers transports that differ by added noise or lost rank. A third mode lies outside it, transports that differ by how much data their fit saw: on two published fits separated by ~0.15% of transport error, coverage orders by fit sample size rather than by transport accuracy, consistently and with the sign inverted. That is a third mode, and coverage's calibration leaves it out.

These are internal checks — the instrument against its own nulls and constructions, with ground truth constructed to a known size rather than assumed. The reads characterise a transport; none of them scores an individual token, and none is offered as a confidence signal.
