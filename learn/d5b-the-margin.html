Entroptics / lesson D5b · the tension under the floor

The Margin

Unit D5a fixed the floor by counting: κ0 = ¼ ln 3 = 0.274653, the same number at every coupling. This unit is the other side of the inequality.

The symbols here, said aloud: κ is kappa, μ is mu, β is beta and θ is theta, the angle a screen resolves. ≥ is greater than or equal, ≤ is less than or equal, ≈ is approximately equal, and ∞ is infinity.

μ(β)  <  κ0   at every β

μ is the centre-vortex tension — the action cost per unit area of a vortex surface. The floor is what those surfaces gain from being numerous. When the tension stays under the floor, the surfaces condense, the flux confines, and the gap inherits the surplus:

Δ  ≥  κ0 − μ(β)

β is the coupling: one dial that sets how strongly the field interacts with itself. It is defined as β = 2N/g², with g the interaction strength — so β large means g small, and the weak end of the theory sits at the large-β end of every table here. Four Greek letters carry this whole argument: κ the floor, μ the cost, β the coupling, Δ the gap.

The coupling axis is covered in three pieces, and they are three different kinds of statement. That is the part worth carrying away.

Strong coupling: a bound you can evaluate

At small β there is a way of writing the theory as a sum that converges — the character expansion — and it gives the tension directly. This is the Osterwalder–Seiler bound. I1 and I2 are two standard functions, the modified Bessel functions; a table or a calculator gives them, and what matters here is that their ratio is a definite number at each β:

μ(β)  ≤  2β · I2(β) ÷ I1(β)

A bound written as a formula is a bound you can put numbers into. Doing that is the whole slide: evaluate it, and find where it crosses the floor.

Weak coupling: the tension goes to zero

At the other end asymptotic freedom does the work. The vortex tension becomes a physical scale measured in lattice units, so it carries a factor of the spacing squared and shrinks as the lattice is refined. Read at fixed lattice size, the free-field tension falls as 1 / L²:

μ∞(L)  ≈  2.1 ÷ L²

By L = 8 the tension is 0.0328, about 8 times under the floor, and it keeps falling. The coefficient is independent of N, so the same statement covers SU(2) and SU(3) together.

This end is where the two theories part company. For SU(N) the tension shrinks as the coupling weakens and stays under the floor. For compact U(1) the monopole action grows with β, crosses the floor at a finite βc, and the theory deconfines. Same floor, opposite running — and unit D5c measures both.

The interior: one inequality, three steps

Between the two ends the bound comes from the aperture itself. The tension is read as a cosine average over the vortex angle distribution, and the chain has three steps, each an inequality in the safe direction.

μ  =  −log ⟨cos θ⟩

One — the aperture fixes the angle scale. A screen of side L resolves angles in steps of 2π/(L+1), so the angular spread inherits the correlation moment ⟨d²⟩ at that scale:

⟨θ²⟩  =  (2π ÷ (L+1))² · ⟨d²⟩

Two — the analytic step. The cosine is bounded below by a parabola for every real x, so an average of cosines is bounded by the average of the parabola:

cos x  ≥  1 − x²/2   ⇒   ⟨cos θ⟩  ≥  1 − ⟨θ²⟩/2

Three — the logarithm carries it through. −log is decreasing, so a lower bound on the cosine average is an upper bound on μ:

μ  ≤  −log(1 − ⟨θ²⟩/2)

Which leaves one measured quantity: the correlation moment ⟨d²⟩. Everything else is geometry and calculus.

Read the first row before the others. At L = 8 the uniform input gives μ ≤ 0.2793, which sits above the floor: the bound holds, and at this size it leaves the tension room to reach the floor. The uniform input clears from L = 12 upward, and L = 12–28 is the window the forward read is taken across.

The reason is in the scaling. ⟨θ²⟩ falls as 1/L², so μ does too, and the factor by which the tension sits under the floor grows as L². From L = 12 to L = 28 that factor grows 5.2×, against a size ratio squared of 5.4.

The measured moments are far smaller than the uniform input: the runtime read finds ⟨d²⟩ between 0.014 and 0.16 across the grid. At the top of that range the bound gives μ ≤ 0.0398 at L = 8 — comfortably under the floor at every size on the table. The uniform input is the one the proof carries, and it is deliberately looser than what is measured.

The one inequality worth checking

Step two of that chain replaced a cosine with a parabola:

cos x  ≥  1 − x²÷2

It holds for every real x, and it is the only step in the whole argument a reader has to take on trust. So here it is at the angles this unit actually uses — reproducible on any calculator:

The parabola sits under the cosine every time, and that direction is what makes the chain safe: replacing the cosine with something smaller can only make the bound on μ larger. An upper bound that errs upward is still an upper bound.

Every inequality in this unit points the same way. The character bound is an upper bound on μ, the parabola makes it looser, and the conclusion is that even the loosened version stays under the floor. A margin that survives being weakened is a margin you can rely on.

The coupling axis, covered

  • Strong coupling is a cited bound, evaluated. 2β I2/I1 stays under the floor for β < 0.7497, and the expansion it comes from is certified convergent to 0.97 — so the bound is used with room to spare.
  • Weak coupling is a limit, computed. The free-field tension falls as 1/L² and is 8× under the floor by L = 8, independently of N.
  • The interior is a theorem with one measured input. The aperture scaling and cos x ≥ 1 − x²/2 turn a bound on ⟨d²⟩ into a bound on μ, and the margin's factor grows as L².
  • Say which size a uniform bound clears at. With ⟨d²⟩ ≤ 1 the interior bound clears from L = 12, not from the smallest size anyone might read. The measured moments clear everywhere on the table.

Every figure here was evaluated from the formula beside it. The strong-coupling threshold has a rigorous counterpart in research/code/certify/beta_star_enclosure.py, which certifies the same bracket in exact rational arithmetic.
