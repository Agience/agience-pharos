Entroptics / lesson two · the optics

Lesson two · the optics

The aperture
reads.

The first lesson built a screen and split it into layers, then asked one question of them: how much of this is real? The answer was a count — K.

This lesson asks a different question of the same object, and gets back a handful of numbers that describe its shape:

What shape is the signal?

Not how big, not how strong — what shape. Is it concentrated or spread? Does one pattern dominate, or many? How much of the room it was given is it actually occupying?

And the surprise is that these turn out to be the same quantities an optician measures about a lens — not by analogy, but the identical arithmetic. That is where the name comes from.

What you already have

the screen S whitened, folded the layers s₁ ≥ s₂ ≥ … the floor σ(√N + √F) the count K

All of it from lesson one, and all of it built from squares, fractions, logs and square roots. Nothing new is needed here either.

Three symbols above, in case you have arrived here first.

σ is small sigma, the Greek s, used everywhere in statistics for the typical size of one noisy value.
√ is the square root — √9 = 3.
≥ is greater than or equal to, so the strengths never increase down the stack though two may tie.

The same two records

A · the scan — one handwritten 0, 8 × 8.
B · the market — eight companies, sixteen months of change, 16 × 8.
Plus two larger ones to show the reads travelling: a planted radio flash and an MRI slice.

Act I · The fill

The same arithmetic, pointed at a new list.

In lesson one you did this four times without being told it was one procedure:

1 square everything → power 2 divide by the total → shares 3 entropy → H bits 4 raise 2 to it → 2H, a count

You fed it the column powers and it told you how many channels were in play. The procedure does not care what list you feed it. So feed it the layer strengths.

Square them, turn them into shares, take the entropy, raise 2 to it. Out comes how many layers are really in play — which is a completely different question from how many channels are, and is answered by the identical four lines of arithmetic.

Then divide by how many layers there could have been. That fraction has a name and a symbol, and it is the centre of this whole lesson.

The fill fraction

φ = 2H ÷ n H from the LAYER strengths n how many layers there are

The symbol. φ is the Greek letter phi — most people rhyme it with “fly”. It is their letter f, and it is used here for fill.

In optics it is the standard name for how much of an aperture is actually letting light through, which is exactly what it is doing for us.

Careful — this is not the fraction from lesson one. That one, 2H/F, came from the column powers and described the axis. This one comes from the layer strengths and describes the modes. Same recipe, different ingredient, different meaning. On the MRI they differ by more than twenty times.

Act I · The fill

What the two ends of φ look like.

Because φ is a fraction of a count, it is trapped between two values you can reason out without a calculator.

The floor: one layer does everything

If the grid is a single multiplication table, there is one non-zero strength and the rest are zero. The entropy of “all the power in one place” is 0 bits, so 20 = 1, and

φ = 1 ÷ n the smallest it can be

Built a rank-1 grid, 40 × 12, and measured it: φ = 0.0833. And 1 ÷ 12 = 0.0833. Exactly the floor, as it must be.

The ceiling: every layer equal

If all n strengths are the same, the entropy is log2 n, so 2H = n and φ = 1. Nothing stands out; every direction is as good as every other.

Pure noise, 40 × 12: φ = 0.856 — near the ceiling, because noise has no preferred shape.

The same fact upside down: reach

magnification = 1 ÷ φ the scan 3.37 the flash 1.46 the market 2.10 an MRI 54.52

If φ is the fraction in use, its reciprocal says how many times over the record is carrying the same information. The MRI at 54.5 is fifty-four-fold redundant — and that number, not any judgement about pictures, is why it compresses.

So φ reads backwards from what you might guess. A small fill is a strong, simple signal. A fill near 1 means there is no shape to find. The flash sits at 0.69 because most of its screen genuinely is noise — only three layers of it were signal.

Act II · Two axes, one area

Do it down each axis, and you have a width and a height.

So far φ described the layers as a whole. But a layer has two halves — a pattern down the rows and a pattern across the columns — and each can be filled to a different degree.

So run the same read twice more: once on how the ordered axis is filled, once on the channels. Two fractions, each between 0 and 1:

φT how filled the ordered axis is φF how filled the channels are

For the market:

φT = 0.3161 φF = 0.4816

Read that as a sentence. The market's sixteen months are more concentrated than its eight companies. A third of the available time-patterns are in play against nearly a half of the company-patterns — which is what you would expect of a record whose big events are a couple of months long and hit everybody.

Meanwhile φT = 0.36, far lower, because the flash really did happen at one moment. Two numbers, and they have located the signal on one axis and not the other.

Act II · Two axes, one area

Multiply them, and the answer is an area.

You have a width and a height, both fractions. Multiply:

E = φT × φF market: 0.3161 × 0.4816 = 0.1522

The word. E here stands for étendue — a French word, said “ay-TON-doo”, meaning extent or spread. Opticians have used it for two centuries, and papers usually write it as a decorated capital E.

It is an area, in the plainest sense: fraction of the time axis times fraction of the channel axis is the fraction of the whole rectangle the signal occupies. The market fills about 15% of its available shape.

Why opticians care

Étendue is conserved. You cannot build a lens that makes a beam both narrower and less spread out — squeeze one and the other grows. It is the reason a magnifying glass cannot concentrate sunlight past a limit, and the reason you cannot funnel a room's light down a fibre without losing some.

Here it is the same statement about information: a record cannot be made both briefer and narrower-band without something being thrown away.

Act II · Two axes, one area

How many spots could fit — and how many are filled.

The étendue is a fraction. Multiply it by how many cells the screen actually has and you get a count of independent things the record can hold.

capacity = nT × nF the space-bandwidth filled = E × capacity

Opticians call the capacity the number of resolvable spots: how many separate points a lens can distinguish without them blurring into each other. It is the honest measure of what an instrument could record.

And the gap between the two is the story. Capacity is what the grid can carry; filled is what is on it. A record that fills most of its capacity is noise-like. One that fills almost none of it is highly structured — which is the same thing as saying it would compress well.

Note the capacity is a property of the grid, not the signal: an unfolded 256 × 148 screen reports 37 888 spots whether it holds a photograph or static.

The flash is mostly noise, and noise needs every spot it can get. The MRI is a picture, and a picture is enormously repetitive — the whole of it can be said with about thirty independent things. Two numbers said that, and neither one looked at a pixel.

Act III · Quality

One more number: does a single pattern dominate?

φ counts how many patterns are active. It does not say whether one of them is enormous. That is a separate question with a separate answer.

Take the ordered axis, list how much power sits in each of its patterns, and ask what share the biggest one holds:

S = λ1 ÷ ( λ1 + λ2 + … )

The symbol. λ is the Greek letter lambda — said “LAM-da”, their letter l. Here each λ is the power in one pattern, and λ1 is the biggest of them.

Worked on the scan: its ordered patterns hold 5.7099 out of a total of 8.0000, so

S = 5.7099 ÷ 8 = 0.7137

71% of everything the scan does down the page is one single pattern. For the market it is 5.9988 ÷ 16 = 0.3749 — far less dominated, because eight companies over sixteen months genuinely do several things at once.

What opticians use this for

This ratio is the Strehl ratio, and in optics it is the number for lens quality. A perfect lens puts all the light from a star into one tight spot: Strehl 1. A flawed one smears it, and the peak drops.

Telescopes are specified by it. Above about 0.8 a lens is called diffraction-limited — as good as physics allows.

Here the “light” is the record’s power and the “spot” is its strongest pattern, so the number means: how much of what happened was one thing happening?

Act III · Quality

Four more, all of them shapes of the same spectrum.

Strehl asked what share the biggest pattern holds. Four further reads ask related questions of the same list of strengths, and each is a plain fraction or ratio you could work out yourself.

top share biggest ÷ total dominance how far the biggest stands above an even spread contrast biggest ÷ the noise floor resolved power how much power sits above that floor

They separate cases that a single number would blur together. A record can have one large pattern and still be mostly noise; another can have no large pattern at all and still be entirely signal.

Contrast is the one to keep. It compares the biggest strength against the floor lesson one derived — so it is the only read here that knows what noise looks like. Below 1 there is nothing to see; the flash reads 2.34, the MRI 26.10.

Both are true at once: the scan is dominated by one pattern, and that pattern still does not clear the floor for a grid holding only 64 numbers. Dominance is about shape; contrast is about evidence.

Act III · Quality

Two different meanings of “concentrated”.

Here is a distinction worth the slide, because it is the kind of thing that quietly ruins a measurement.

Think of every row of the screen as an arrow. Ask “are these arrows concentrated?” and there are two completely different questions hiding in it:

  • Do they lie along one line? — the axial question. Call it focus.
  • Do they point the same way? — the directional question. Call it the resultant: just the average arrow’s length.

These are not the same, and one example settles it. Take a cloud where half the arrows point one way and half point exactly the opposite way — all along one line.

focus = 1.0000 perfectly axial resultant = 0.000000 they cancel

Measured on exactly that cloud: 20 copies of an arrow and 20 of its opposite. Maximum focus, zero resultant. Anyone reporting a single “concentration” number would have to pick one and be wrong about the other.

Why it matters here. A signal that swings symmetrically about zero — a market that goes down as hard as it goes up, an oscillation, an alternating pattern — is strongly concentrated on one axis and has no net direction at all. Reporting only the resultant would call it structureless. It is not.

Act IV · Why these names

None of this was borrowed. It is the same arithmetic.

It would be easy to think the optical words are a decoration — a nice metaphor bolted onto some statistics. They are not. Each read is the same formula an optician writes about a real aperture, and each carries a named theorem behind it.

what we computedwhat it is called in optics
2H of the layer strengths, over naperture fill fraction
1 ÷ φreach, or oversampling
φT × φFétendue — the Lagrange invariant
nT × nFspace–bandwidth product — resolvable spots

the autocorrelation of the recordthe point-spread function  → lesson 3
its entropy widththe diffraction limit, Abbe and Rayleigh  → lesson 3

The right-hand column is a list of things measured in laboratories since the 1870s. The left-hand column is what you get by squaring numbers, turning them into shares and taking a logarithm.

The last two rows are not in this lesson. Every read here was taken with the record standing still — nothing above ever looked at the order the rows came in. The point-spread function and the diffraction limit both come from comparing rows with later rows, so they need the ordered axis and they get their own lesson.

That is lesson three: the point-spread — how long a record remembers, how finely it can resolve, and how fast each layer fades.

Why they coincide. An optical aperture is a finite window that light passes through, and what comes out the far side is limited by the window’s size and shape. A record is a finite window too — so many rows, so many channels — and what you can learn from it is limited the same way.

The mathematics does not know whether the window is made of glass. It only knows the window is finite.

What the whole thing bought

Lesson one turned a grid into a screen and asked how much is real. It answered with a count.

Lesson two asked what shape is it, and answered with a handful of dimensionless fractions — every one of them between 0 and 1, every one of them comparable between a handwriting scan and a radio telescope.

That comparability is the entire point. Not one of these numbers carries a unit, a threshold, or a setting.

Lesson three asks the one question neither of these two has touched: what does the record do as you move down the ordered axis?

Your turn

Three problems, and one thing to notice.

1 · Find the fill

A screen has 5 layers. Their strengths are 6, 0, 0, 0, 0. Without a calculator, what is φ?

Answer

All the power is in one layer, so the shares are 1, 0, 0, 0, 0 and the entropy is 0 bits. 20 = 1, and φ = 1 ÷ 5 = 0.2 — the floor for a 5-layer screen. The grid is one multiplication table.

2 · Find the area

A record measures φT = 0.25 and φF = 0.40, on a grid whose capacity is 800 spots. What is its étendue, and how many spots is it filling?

Answer

E = 0.25 × 0.40 = 0.10 — it occupies a tenth of its available shape.
Filled = 0.10 × 800 = 80 spots of the 800 it could have used.

3 · Read the shape

Two records, same size grid. One reads φ = 0.92, the other φ = 0.11. Which one would compress well, and which is probably mostly noise?

Answer

φ = 0.11 compresses well: nearly all its power sits in a few layers, so a few layers describe it. φ = 0.92 is close to the ceiling, meaning every direction carries about as much as every other — the signature of noise, and the reason noise cannot be compressed.

And the thing to notice. Every number in this lesson is a fraction. Not one of them has a unit. Not one of them needed a threshold, a setting, or a decision from you.

That is what let a 64-number handwriting scan and a 37 888-spot hospital image appear in the same table on the same scale — and it came from nothing more exotic than squaring, dividing by a total, and taking a logarithm.
