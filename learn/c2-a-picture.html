A Record That Is a Picture

Track C is about records that arrive as something other than a table. A photograph is the case where a reader already knows what the record is, so every number the instrument returns can be checked against something they can see.

The structure is the picture's. Shuffling the rows destroys it and a record built to contain nothing has none of it — which is worth establishing before reading three findings off a page of large numbers.

Three colours, one mode

A photograph has three colour channels. Take each image row's average colour and you have a record 600 rows tall and three columns wide — red, green, blue.

Now ask the two questions units A12 and A15 separate:

questionreadanswer
how many columns are carrying the record?2H of the power share2.9681 of 3
how many modes stand above the floor?Ksignal 1

The shares are 0.3549, 0.2669, 0.3782 — near enough a third each, so all three channels are carrying. And one mode stands up.

Both are right, and a reader who has looked at a colour photograph knows why. Red, green and blue each hold about a third of the energy, and they are near-copies of one another: where the picture is bright all three are bright. Three columns working; one thing being said.

This is the clearest case in the course for why the two counts are published separately. On the EEG or the market you would have to take it on trust that a count of columns and a count of modes answer different questions. Here you can see it.

Blur it, and watch the resolution

Average the pixels in k × k blocks. That is a blur, and everyone knows what it does to a photograph: it takes away the fine detail and leaves the shape. Unit B5's spot count says how much:

87.8037 · 43.6731 · 21.8445 · 11.2519

Each doubling of the blur halves the spots. The three ratios are 2.010, 1.999, 1.941, against a doubling of k every row. That is what the word resolution means, arrived at by measurement.

Which is the point of doing this on a picture. The spot count is defined from the entropy of a record's own decay — nothing in that definition mentions blurring, pixels or photographs. Put it against a manipulation whose effect a reader can see, and it returns the answer they would have predicted.

Turned on its side

Unit E3 read a terrain grid in two directions and got two answers. A photograph makes the reason plain: up-and-down and left-and-right are different things in a picture of a person standing up.

read downshapeKsignal contrastspotsz
the rows, as stored600 × 51216 52.708987.80+74.121
the columns, turned512 × 6001632.6172 61.53+46.307

The mode count agrees and everything else moves. Both readings find 16 modes; the spot count runs 87.80 against 61.53 and the coherence +74.121 against +46.307.

Neither is the correct reading. They answer how does the picture change as you go down and as you go across, and in a portrait those are different questions — a face has more going on vertically than a shoulder does horizontally.

So a spot count quoted without the direction it was read in is an answer with the question thrown away, which is unit A11's rule and unit E3's finding on a record where the reason is visible.

What a picture is for

  • Three columns working, one mode standing. 2.9681 of the three colour channels carry the record and Ksignal is 1. Both counts are right, and the reason is something a reader can see: red, green and blue are near-copies of each other.
  • Blur halves the resolution. 87.8037 spots becomes 43.6731, 21.8445, 11.2519 as the blur doubles. Nothing in the spot count's definition mentions pixels, and it returns the answer a reader would have predicted.
  • Direction is part of the question. 87.80 spots down the rows, 61.53 down the columns, on the same picture.
  • The structure is the record's. Shuffled rows read -1.4948 to +0.8269 and noise of the same shape reads Ksignal 0.

Every read in this unit is one an earlier unit built on the EEG or on eight companies' share prices, where a reader has to take the meaning on trust. A photograph is the control for the whole A-track: put the instrument on a record whose structure you already know, and see whether it agrees with you.
