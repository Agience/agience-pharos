The claim, in one sentence

Some particles have mass, and for one family of forces it has never been shown from first principles why. The claim here is that the answer is an aperture effect — the same idea unit A9 measured on a wide field.

An observer with a finite window can only resolve so much. A finite window band-limits what gets through. And a band-limited thing has its predictive excess fall away at some positive rate. That positive rate is the gap.

finite aperture → band-limited → a positive decay rate

Where the number comes from

The claim puts a floor under the rate: it is at least a particular size, and that size comes from counting. Counting what: at each step a closed surface has three ways to continue, and the choice is spread across four faces. Three equally likely options carry ln 3 of entropy; sharing that across four faces gives a quarter of it per face:

κ0  =  ¼ × ln 3  =  0.274653

κ is the Greek letter kappa, said KAP-uh. ln is the natural logarithm — ln 3 is the entropy of a three-way choice. Nothing was measured to obtain that value; it is arithmetic, and it is checked here. Every other number on the following slides is read out of the repository.

So the claim takes its sharp form: the decay rate is at least κ0.

The same statement, written as a magnitude

The table on the next slide reports a magnitude per step, written mhi. A magnitude and a rate are the same statement in different clothes. A quantity decaying at rate κ is multiplied by e−κ at every step, so a bigger rate is a smaller multiplier:

rate ≥ κ0  ⇔  magnitude ≤ e−κ0  =  3−¼  =  0.7598

That is one number in two forms. A floor on the rate is a ceiling on the magnitude, and the ceiling is what the table's last column tests. Reading a row as under the ceiling is reading it as above the floor; there is nothing else to remember about the arithmetic of this unit.

The chain, and where each link is walked

The argument reaches the gap through one inequality. Everything else is establishing its two sides and carrying the result to a spectrum:

Δ  ≥  κ0 − μ(β)  >  0

κ0 is what vortex surfaces gain from being numerous, and μ(β) is what one costs. When the cost stays under the gain the surfaces condense, the flux confines to a tube, and the screen's excess decays at a rate the margin bounds from below.

Why this is an optics argument

The paper's own title calls the mass gap a finite-aperture effect. That is the A-track's subject, and the correspondence is exact — every idea on the left was measured in an earlier unit.

a finite aperture cannot resolve arbitrarily fine detail (A9)a band-limit: there is a finest mode the screen can carry
the point-spread: how influence falls off along an ordered axis (A3, C4)the correlation falling off per step along the time axis
modes above a floor, counted (A1, A2, A7) how many directions the transport actually uses, against the confined-vacuum floor
too little record to answer (A10) the lattice too small to resolve the rate — the L = 8 row in D5d

And the argument itself, in three lines:

  • A theory with no gap needs a mode of unlimited reach — something that stays correlated however far apart you look.
  • A finite aperture has nowhere to put one. A window of finite size carries finitely many modes, and none of them reaches forever.
  • So what the screen has left over decays, and the rate it decays at is the gap.

Which is why the counting in D5a matters at all. It fixes how fast that decay must be for the picture to hold, and the four units after this one establish the floor, bound the cost against it, measure both on real fields, and turn the surviving rate into a statement about energies.

The error bar that grew

Here is the finding, and it is the best sentence in the whole repository. Someone regenerated one row with more data — and the uncertainty got bigger:

at β=2.50, L=16Δerror

“adding 32 configurations made an error bar six times larger… More data does not do that. Mixed data does.”

That is a complete diagnosis in eight words. Adding samples of the same thing narrows an estimate. An estimate that widens has been fed samples of two different things.

Why widening means mixture

Take many measurements of one quantity. They scatter around its value, and the average of n of them scatters less — by a factor of 1 ÷ √n. Every extra configuration buys a little more certainty. The error bar can only shrink.

Now take measurements of two quantities that sit apart, and label them all as one. The scatter now reports the distance between them, which is a property of the two populations and holds however many samples are drawn. Adding more of the second one widens the mixture: it moves the sample toward a two-humped shape whose spread is set by the gap between the humps.

So the direction of the change is the whole signal. Narrowing is uninformative — it is what both cases can do early on. Widening has only one cause, and it is not a shortage of data.

And so it proved. The stored configurations came from three collections. Two pairs of them, at the same box size and the same coupling, sit 21.0 and 57.6 standard deviations apart — written σ, the Greek letter sigma, said SIG-muh, the usual shorthand for how much a measurement ordinarily wobbles — about ten times the spread within either one, so the separation is systematic. Nothing recorded distinguishes them: same shapes, same data type, same nominal coupling, and the generation notes describe all three identically.

Pooling dilutes the anomaly

One coupling in the ensemble reads anomalously. Pooling the collections together moves the average toward the ordinary ones and the anomaly stops showing.

24 anomalous configurations among 96 ordinary ones

That is dilution: the anomalous configurations are still there and still anomalous, and the pooled average is dominated by the majority. The size of the minority is what decides whether an average can carry the question — the same arithmetic that makes a uniform sample across unequal groups report the majority's answer.

How much of the anomaly survives pooling

Work it out. The anomalous configurations are 24 of 120 — a share of 0.20. An average is the share-weighted sum of what it averages, so if the anomalous group sits a distance d away from the ordinary one, the pooled average sits 0.20 × d away:

24 ÷ (24 + 96)  =  0.20  —  an anomaly of size d reads as 0.20 d

So the pool reports 20% of what the minority actually shows, and reports it against the full sample's error bar. The measurement was made on a population where the property being looked for sits in the minority, and the pooled average reports it at that weight.

This is why the share is the number to ask for, not the count. 24 anomalous configurations sounds like a substantial finding. 20% of the sample tells you the weight that finding carries in an average of that sample.

What “machine-checked” buys you

The mathematics is written out in a language a computer verifies line by line — 43 files, 10,552 lines. Checked here: it contains 0 unfinished proof steps.

Each one is an established result from elsewhere in physics, named so that a reader can see exactly what is being leaned on. The claim is therefore: given these, the rest follows, and a machine has checked that it does.

What this unit establishes is what the files say. They were read and counted; running the toolchain is a separate act, and judging whether the assumptions are the right ones is a physics question. Both sit outside this measurement.

The count above distinguishes an unfinished step from the word for one. All 4 occurrences in this development sit inside a comment stating there are none, and the search removes comments before it counts.

How that search is written decides what it can miss, and it was written the wrong way round until 2026-08-28. It matched each line against a whitelist of three shapes — a bare sorry, a sorry,, or := sorry — and filed everything else as prose. A whitelist enumerates forms, so it misses the ones nobody thought of: exact sorry, · sorry, by sorry, or a sorry indented on the line after := would each have been dismissed as a comment. It now strips comments and reads every line that remains, over the vocabulary sorry, sorryAx, admit, so every occurrence of those words reaches the count.

Both rules give the same answer here, and that is the point worth taking. The finding did not change; what changed is whether it was established or merely consistent with a rule that could have looked past it. And the claim is now stated as what was actually done: not “no unfinished proof steps”, which nothing short of the compiler can settle, but none of those three words appears outside a comment in 43 files.
