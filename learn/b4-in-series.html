Two Lenses
in Series

Unit B1 certified one lens. Nothing in a working system is one lens — a signal passes through a conversion, then a routing step, then another conversion, and what arrives has been through all of them.

So: which of those certificates survive being chained? The answer is not the same for each, and one of them turns out to have a closed form.

Everything here runs on the record this course has carried since unit A3, and the lenses are the ones unit B1 built — a rotation and a scaling that each certify clean, and a rank truncation that keeps every joule while losing a direction.

Two clean lenses in series stay clean. Each certifies at machine precision (1.27e-16 and 5.32e-17), and the pair certifies at 1.42e-16 — the error does not accumulate into anything the instrument can see.

And one lossy link poisons the chain wherever it sits. There is no arrangement of the others that recovers it, which is the whole point: what the truncation removed was structure, and nothing downstream can put back what is not there.

Look at the last row. Applying the truncation twice gives exactly the residual of applying it once. It is idempotent — which puts it with unit B3's authority meet, where intersecting twice gives what intersecting once gave, and against B3's optical rate, where distance keeps costing you.

How far it composes

The chain on the previous slide is two links. A working pipeline is not two, so the same clean links were run over and over — and the two operations run apart as well as together, because “the chain got long” and “the links amplify” are different diagnoses and only measuring them separately tells them apart.

The scaling multiplies by 3 at every link. At 64 links it has magnified the record by a factor of 364, and the round trip still certifies at 5.2e-16. Gain is not what breaks a chain.

The rotation does break it, and early. Its matrix has a condition number of just 3.30 — unremarkable, the kind of number nobody flags in review — and the chain stops certifying at 9 links, at a residual of 2.7e-13.

Both together break at the same depth as the rotation alone. The scaling, for all its gain, contributes nothing to the failure.

residual × 2.92 per link   ·   condition number 3.30

Between 4 and 16 links the residual grows by a factor of 2.92 for every link added, measured. Hold that against the link's condition number, 3.30: the growth sits at or below it, which is what conditioning compounding looks like. A chain of n links inherits about the n-th power of one link's conditioning, and the arithmetic runs out when that reaches the size of the record itself.

The two numbers are close and they are not the same number, which is the honest picture: the condition number is a worst case over every direction, and the record only explores some of them. A measured growth under the bound is the bound doing its job.

So the claim from the previous slide holds with a qualification worth having. Losslessness composes until the composed map's conditioning exhausts the arithmetic, and how soon that happens is set by the conditioning of each link — not by how many links there are, and not by how much they amplify. Which means the number to look at when a long pipeline starts drifting is not its length.

Order changes the result

The same two operations, swapped. Nothing else changes — same record, same lenses, same instrument:

orderresidualdeparture from linearity
rotation, then the lossy step0.7307990.712854
the lossy step, then rotation0.7294520.778465

The difference is small and it is systematic: the rotation changes which direction is the strongest one before the truncation chooses what to keep. Rotate first and a different direction survives.

This is the practical reason a pipeline's stages cannot be reordered for convenience — not even two steps that both “look linear”. Composition does not commute, and the instrument can measure that it does not.

Which is also a warning about benchmarking a chain. Move a step and you have not tuned the same system; you have measured a different one. Unit D2 has the version of that mistake where a comparison was rigged by giving one side a screen the other did not get.

Nonlinearity compounds, and you can predict it

Chain a nonlinear step with itself and the departure grows. The library measures homogeneity by scaling the input by c = 2 and comparing against scaling the output, so for a lens that raises its input to the power p the departure should be exactly:

homogeneity  =  (cp − c) / c

Squaring departs by 1. Squaring twice — a fourth power — by 7. Once more and it is 127. Every one agrees with the formula to the last digit.

That agreement is a different kind of evidence from the rest of this course, and worth naming. Most figures here are checked against the last time they were measured. These are checked against a formula derived without measuring anything — so if the library's definition of homogeneity ever changed, this slide would break in a way that re-running it could not repair.

What to check when you build a chain

losslesscomposes to a depth — clean links stay clean, one dirty link is fatal to the whole chain, and the depth at which even clean links stop certifying is set by their conditioning (9 links here, at a condition number of 3.30)

a rank truncationidempotent — twice is once

The practical rule is short. Certify each link, then certify the chain, because the second is not implied by the first in the direction that matters: every link passing is necessary and the chain passing is what you actually rely on.

And the honest limit. These are five lenses on one record, run to a depth of 64. Composition is a structural claim and this is evidence for it, not a proof — the library certifies whatever chain you hand it, and the cheap thing is to hand it yours and read what it certifies.
