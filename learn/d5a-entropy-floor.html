The Entropy Floor

The whole mass-gap argument turns on one comparison: what a thing costs against how many of that thing there are. This unit works out the second number, and every step of it is arithmetic you can do on a calculator.

The symbols here, said aloud: κ is kappa, the floor this unit counts out; μ is mu, what one plaquette of sheet costs; ξ is xi. ≥ is greater than or equal and ∞ is infinity, which appears under a limit.

The number is called κ0, and it comes from counting shapes on a grid. No physics goes into it — no coupling, no choice of scale, nothing measured. It is a piece of geometry, and the next slide gives you the five words you need to see what is being counted.

κ0  =  0.274653

That is where this unit ends up. What follows is where it comes from.

Paper §7, Theorem 7.1. The Lean development is research/lean/MassGap/Floor.lean.

The five words

The argument counts a particular kind of object, so it is worth having the words first. Each of these is one line.

wordwhat it is
latticespace chopped into a grid of cubes, so that it fits in a computer. Unit A1's grid of measurements, in four dimensions
plaquetteone square face of one cube. The lattice's unit of area — areas here are counted in plaquettes the way a floor is counted in tiles
vortexa closed sheet made of plaquettes, carrying a twist in the field. Closed means it has no edge, like the surface of a balloon
tension μwhat one plaquette of that sheet costs. A big sheet costs μ per plaquette, so it is suppressed by e−μA
floor κ0how many such sheets there are, per plaquette. Many sheets of the same area means many chances, which works against the cost

The last two are the two sides of one comparison, and everything in this unit is about the second. Cost against count. If the sheets are numerous enough to outweigh what each one costs, they fill space, and that filling is what confinement is.

You do not need to know what a vortex is made of to follow the counting. What matters is its shape: a closed surface built out of unit squares, and the question is how many closed surfaces of a given area there are.

The comparison, written down

Now that the words are in place, here is the whole thing in one line. A sheet of area A counts twice over: its cost makes it less likely by a factor e−μA, and the number of sheets of that area makes it more likely by a factor e+κA. Subtract the two rates:

Fv  =  μ − κ

When that is negative — when μ < κ — the count wins, the sheets fill space, and the flux is confined. So a lower bound on κ is worth having: it lets the whole question be settled by measuring μ alone, which is what unit D5b does.

Count a family you can count

N(A) is the number of closed connected centre-vortex surfaces of area A through one fixed plaquette. Counting all of them is hard. Exhibiting a large family of them and counting that gives a lower bound, and a lower bound is what the argument needs.

The family: take a path of cubes in a fixed three-dimensional sublattice, each cube touching the last face to face, and use the boundary of the resulting solid. The boundary of any solid is a closed surface, so every member of the family qualifies.

The paths are directed: every step moves in +x, +y or +z, never backwards. That single restriction earns two things at once.

  • They are self-avoiding for free. Each step raises the coordinate sum x+y+z by exactly one, so the sum is different at every cube and no position can repeat. A general path would need a check; this family needs none.
  • They are easy to count. A path of n cubes is fixed by its n−1 steps, each a free choice among three. So there are 3n−1 of them, and no two are the same path.

In Lean that count is directed_paths_card: the paths are the maps Fin k → Fin 3, and there are 3 ^ k of them.

The area of the boundary

Now the area, in plaquettes. Work it out on the first two cases and the rule appears.

One cube. A cube has six faces and there is nothing for it to share one with, so the boundary is all six: A = 6, and the enumeration agrees.

Two cubes. The second cube brings six more faces, and the face where the two meet is now inside the solid — so it leaves the boundary, and it leaves for both cubes. Six added, two removed: A = 10.

Every further cube does exactly the same thing. Starting from six and adding four each time:

A  =  6n − 2(n−1)  =  4n + 2

Which is the number worth holding on to: each cube adds four plaquettes of area, and carries three choices with it. Those two numbers are the whole theorem.

Different paths, different surfaces

The count 3n−1 is a count of paths. The theorem needs a count of surfaces. Those agree only if two different paths can never produce the same boundary, so that step is the one to check.

They cannot, and the reason is the same coordinate sum that made the paths self-avoiding. Along a path the cubes carry sums 0, 1, 2, …, n−1, one each. So the solid remembers its own path: sort the cubes by coordinate sum and the path comes back. A surface that came from two different paths would need its cubes in two different orders, and there is only one order.

The map from path to surface is therefore injective, and the surface count is at least the path count:

N(A)  ≥  3n−1  =  3(A−2)/4−1

Watched directly at the largest case enumerated: 2187 paths at n = 8 produce 2187 distinct boundary surfaces, compared as sets of faces. The counts match at every row of the table above.

In Lean this is cubeConfig_injective, proved for every k. The enumeration here shows a reader what the lemma is about, and the lemma covers every case past where an enumeration can go.

What one plaquette is worth

Two numbers came out of the counting, and they are the whole theorem:

per cube addedvalue
ways to continue the path3
plaquettes of boundary added4

Three choices arrive together with four plaquettes. So spread the choices evenly over the plaquettes and ask what one plaquette is worth. Four of them multiply to 3, so each one is the fourth root of 3:

3¼  =  1.316074   choices per plaquette

Check it on a calculator: 1.316074 × 1.316074 × 1.316074 × 1.316074 = 3.0000. Four plaquettes, three choices, exactly as the counting said.

Now turn it around. A vortex surface is suppressed by its action weight and multiplied by how many surfaces there are. For the multiplicity to lose, the weight has to shrink by more than the count grows — so per plaquette it must shrink by more than one part in 1.316074. That reciprocal is the number the published tables are read against:

1  ÷  1.316074  =  0.7598

Unit A7 asked what “nothing” looks like, and unit A1 counted modes standing above a floor. This is the same shape of question with the floor fixed by geometry: how much can a thing shrink and still be outnumbered?

The same number, written as a rate

The previous slide produced two numbers by multiplying and dividing:

per plaquettevaluewhat it counts
3¼1.316074how fast the number of surfaces grows
1 ÷ 3¼0.7598how fast a surface's weight must shrink

Physics writes growth and shrinking as rates, because rates add along a path where factors multiply. The tool for that is the natural logarithm, ln: it answers what exponent produced this? Applied to the growth factor:

κ0  =  ln(3¼)  =  ¼ × ln 3  =  0.274653

And the shrinking factor is the same rate with a minus sign, run back through e:

e−κ0  =  0.7598   — the same 0.7598 as the division above

Two ways of writing one fact. Factors multiply along a path; rates add. That is the only reason the logarithm is here, and unit B3 uses it for exactly the same purpose: survival over a distance is e−d/ξ, a factor per step written as a rate.

Whichever form you prefer, the content is one sentence: a vortex surface carries 1.316074 times as many shapes for every plaquette of area, and its weight has to fall faster than that to stay suppressed.

Entropy per unit area

κ is the growth rate of the count per unit area — how much entropy one plaquette of surface carries:

κ  =  limA→∞   ln N(A) ÷ A

Substituting the family's count and its area, with n cubes giving A = 4n + 2:

ln N(A) ÷ A  ≥  (n−1) ln 3 ÷ (4n + 2)

Take n large. The −1 and the +2 stop mattering, and the ratio settles at ln 3 over 4: three choices, four plaquettes.

What the floor is worth

κ0 carries no coupling and no lattice spacing. It came from counting cubes, and the cubes know nothing about β or about how fine the lattice is. That is what makes it usable at every coupling at once, and what lets it survive the continuum limit unchanged.

Two consequences the rest of the argument leans on.

  • Confinement becomes one inequality. Fv = μ − κ < 0 is implied by μ < κ0, so the whole question is whether the measured tension stays under one fixed number. Unit D5b is that measurement, at both coupling ends and across the interior.
  • The gap inherits the surplus. The margin below the floor is what bounds the decay rate: Δ ≥ κ0 − μ(β). The floor supplies the scale, and it supplies it dimension-free, so the bound holds as modes are added and as the spacing goes to zero.

The same statement, written as a magnitude per step, reads as a ceiling: a rate of at least κ0 is a multiplier of at most:

e−κ0  =  3−¼  =  0.7598

That is the number the published tables are read against, and unit D5 is where they are read.

The counting, end to end

  • Bound a hard count with an easy one. Closed vortex surfaces are hard to enumerate; the boundaries of directed cube-paths are a family inside them that counts itself.
  • Three choices, four plaquettes. Each cube added to a path offers three directions and adds four plaquettes of boundary area — six new faces less the two that stop being on the outside. Those two numbers are κ0.
  • Injectivity is what makes it a count of surfaces. The coordinate sum orders the cubes, so the surface remembers its path, and 3n−1 paths give 3n−1 surfaces.
  • The floor is geometry. No coupling, no spacing, no fit — κ0 = ¼ ln 3 = 0.274653, the same number at every β and every lattice spacing.

Everything on these slides was produced by enumerating the family: 2187 paths at n = 8, each boundary built face by face, each area counted. The general statement is the theorem, and the Lean development carries it for every n.
