The Same Instrument,
Elsewhere

Every unit before this one, and one set of reads: a screen, a spectrum, a floor, a count of what stands above it. All of them have been applied to a table of numbers.

This unit is about three places that are not tables of numbers — deciding what to retrieve, receiving a radio transmission, and a question in theoretical physics — where the same reads turn up doing the work.

Two of the three are measured here end to end. The third belongs to unit D5, which gives a claim about physical reality a unit of its own.

One — how many to keep

Every search system faces the same question: given a pile of candidates ranked by relevance, how many do you actually take? The usual answers are a fixed number, or a threshold, both tuned against a collection that then changes.

The reads in this curriculum already answer that question — it is the same “how many of these are signal” used in units A1, A2 and C4. Here is what happens when relevance is spread across several distinct aspects of a query:

Read the top three rows first. They tie, and a tie there is the correct answer, not a weak one — with a single coherent aspect the ordinary similarity score is the right thing to compute, and a method claiming to beat it would be suspect.

Then it separates. At 4 aspects, 0.806 against 1.000. At 5 and 6, similarity ranking collapses to 0.000 — it finds nothing at all — while the cut is still perfect.

The reason is worth a sentence. A genuinely relevant item matches one aspect strongly and the others not at all, so as aspects multiply its overall score sinks toward the mediocre-at-everything items. One number cannot avoid that. Its pattern, though, is intact the whole time.

Why that table is believable

A benchmark where the author's method wins is worth exactly as much as its fairness, so it is worth saying how this one is built. Both sides get the same input and the same stopping rule. The identical screen is handed to each; similarity takes its row sum, the cut takes its coherent-mode energy; and the same parameter-free lock decides how many to keep on both sides. The only thing under test is whether the screen comes before the lock.

That precision was not free. The benchmark's own notes record an earlier version of this same result that did not do it:

“the cosine side ran a crippled lock, it kept ~1.2 items where the cut kept ~3.6, and the reported ‘+0.038 win’ was the lock rather than the screen. That is the single most important methodological note in this repository.”

A comparison is a claim about two things being alike in every way but one. Almost every disappointing benchmark result is really a difference nobody controlled for.

Two — a radio with no radio in it

A receiver normally has to be told a great deal in advance: the frequency, the timing, the symbol rate, the frame layout. Every one of those is a parameter two parties must agree on before anything can be sent.

Here the receiver is told a key, and nothing else — and for an open broadcast, not even that. Everything else it measures off the signal, using the reads from units A1 and A2. There is no frequency transform anywhere in the path.

Zero errors at every level, down to 5 dB. Not a low rate — none. And the transmission carries 8 modes with a 4-symbol alphabet in a 64-sample aperture, all of which the receiver worked out for itself.

The last two columns are the ones to watch. The receiver's own confidence falls honestly from a margin of 283 to 1.11 as conditions worsen, while the answer stays perfect. It is telling you how close it is to the edge before it gets there.

And the control

“Told a key and nothing else” is only worth something if the key is what did the work. A table of zero-error rows cannot show that: a receiver that locked onto the signal regardless of its key would print exactly the same table. So the same transmission was handed to 20 receivers holding the wrong key, at the strongest signal level in the table — the condition most favourable to a wrong key, and so the hardest place for this to hold.

A wrong key returns a coin. The median error rate is 0.5017 — half the bits wrong, which is what you get by guessing — and not one of the 20 came close to the message. The key is carrying the whole load.

The second row is the one to take away, and it is the same point as the paragraph above. Every one of them produced bits. Every wrong key produced a full payload of confident-looking bits. The margin is what separates them from the real one — 283 against at most 2.59, two orders of magnitude. A caller who read the bits and ignored the margin would be handed noise that looks exactly like data. The instrument says which it is; you have to read the part where it says so.

Fill. Etendue. Strehl. Dominance. Unit A2 measured those on a spreadsheet of share prices and a scan of a handwritten digit. Here they are describing a radio transmission, and they are not analogies — the same functions produced them.

Which is the point of this whole programme, stated as plainly as it can be. The reads were never about spreadsheets, or images, or radio. They are about an ordered record with more than one channel, and that shape turns up in places that share no subject matter at all.

A Strehl of 0.1407 says most of the received energy is not in one dominant mode — which is correct here, and is what carrying 8 modes at once looks like.

What the whole programme was about

One instrument, every unit up to this one. It read a scan and a spreadsheet, found out whether a record was ordered at all, read a record made of letters, put two records on one screen, priced what travel costs, turned words into coordinates, kept them, decided what to forget, looked inside a language model — and then decoded a radio signal and reported fill and Étendue.

  • Derive it, do not choose it. How many to keep, when now ends, how wide the frame — every one measured from the data.
  • An unanswerable question gets an exact zero. Exact zero, silence, unmeasured: six separate reads across the programme land on one of those.
  • Put the control inside the figure. The shuffled record, the scrambled pair, the subtraction that reveals a band — a result you can check without trusting the method.
  • Say where the evidence stops. Two claims in unit D1, and the whole third of this unit.

And the one that cost the most to learn, three separate times while building this: a wrong measurement usually looks fine. An impossible effective rank, a benchmark rigged by accident, a distance computed against the wrong tree — none of them raised an error. Only checking against something already known caught any of them.

Which is why every figure in every one of these units is re-derived from the live code each time the page is built, and the build fails if a single one has moved.

The third destination — a question in theoretical physics, read as an aperture effect — is deliberately not summarised here. It gets the treatment it is owed in unit D5: what it takes to check a claim about physical reality with this instrument, and what the checking actually found.
