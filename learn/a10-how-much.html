Unit A10 · the instrument

How Much
Is Enough?

The question nobody asks until a read comes back empty. Every unit so far has shown you what the instrument says. This one is about when it is entitled to say anything.

Two units meet it on a single record each. Unit A5, earlier in this track, finds a sequence whose entropy falls more steeply than English and carries no structure. The capstone, unit E1, runs a control that resolves nothing across twelve rows at three widths — and the grid below settles which of the two explanations that leaves.

The measurement here plants a signal of known rank 3 in noise, then asks the instrument how many modes it resolves — across a grid of shapes, with 7 seeds in every cell, so that every cell reports a spread.

Read the leftmost column downwards. At four columns the best any number of rows achieves is 2 — eight hundred rows still misses a mode that twenty rows at sixty-four columns finds. Going down the table is the weaker lever, and going down the table is what most people mean by “get more data”.

The cell at 20 rows by 64 columns resolves all 3, from 20 rows. Width is what sets the ceiling: the modes have to be distinguishable in the space they live in, and a narrow record gives them too little room to separate, at any length.

The other lever

The grid holds the noise at 0.30 throughout, which leaves one obvious objection standing: perhaps four columns fail because the noise is loud for them, and a cleaner signal would recover the missing mode. That is a testable claim, so here it is tested — the same narrow shapes, taken down to a noise level 300 times quieter than the grid's.

width → the ceiling   ·   noise → how much of it you reach

Which makes “our data is noisy” and “our data is narrow” two different problems with two different remedies, and the instrument tells them apart. A record that answers the same way however clean it gets is narrow. One that improves the moment it is widened was noisy.

And where it reads zero

One cell in the grid resolved nothing at all, across every seed: 8×4. Not a small count. Zero.

too little record → no answer, not a weak one

That is the zero doing exactly what it is for. A read that returned “0.03 modes” on a record too small to support the question would invite belief in a structure the record cannot evidence. Returning nothing says ask me again with more.

The same grid answers a question left open two units earlier, about a record that was small in one direction and wide in the other.

Twelve rows is enough to carry an answer. Unit E1's positive control ran on a bag of word vectors shaped 12 rows by 512 columns. Plant a rank-3 signal at exactly that shape and this measurement resolves a median of 3 (2 to 3 across 7 seeds) — the full planted rank.

So E1's reading is settled by what its record carries. A record of that shape can hold a signal of that rank, which leaves the explanation E1 gave: these reads are about structure along an ordered axis, and a bag of words is unordered.

The spread is what makes that a measurement. 7 seeds at that shape return a median of 3, running from 2 to 3. A single draw reports one of those numbers and gives no way to tell which one it drew.

When too little record looks like a finding

Too little record can also produce an answer that looks like a strong finding, and that is the more dangerous direction. Unit A5 met it head on.

Reduce a company's monthly moves to three symbols — up, flat, down — and measure how predictable the sequence is as you look at longer and longer blocks. The curve falls more steeply than English. It looks like a discovery.

what there is
symbols in the sequence70
possible blocks of 8, from a 3-symbol alphabet6,561
ratioabout 1 observation per 93 possibilities

Almost every block the estimator sees is one it has never seen before, so it looks perfectly predictable — each block has, in the data, exactly one continuation. A shortage of data reads exactly like structure.

The instrument catches it, and A5 shows how: redundancy comes back exactly 0.0000, the surrogate test finds no onset, and the largest z is +1.12. Two charts sloped downward; one of them meant it.

The rule that falls out is this: ask what your estimator needs before you read its output, and prefer a read that lands on zero when the record cannot carry the question.

What to take away

instinctwhat the grid says
“get more data”usually means more rows, which is the weaker lever — 800 rows at 4 columns resolved 2; 20 rows at 64 resolved 3
“a wide record is expensive”and a narrow one may make the answer unreachable at any length
“an empty result means nothing is there”or that the record could not have shown it. Those are different, and the shape tells you which
“a steep curve is a finding”check what the estimator needed first — starvation and structure look alike

And the scope of it. The noise level was swept and the ceiling held; what stays fixed here is the construction — a low-rank signal in additive noise, read at one instrument setting. On another construction the boundary sits somewhere else. What transfers is the method: plant something you know the answer to, sweep the shape, and find out where your own reads stop working before you rely on one.

Which is the same discipline unit A7 applied to the noise floor, and the same one behind the exact zero. Calibrate the instrument on a case whose answer you already know. It is the cheapest check in this entire course and the one most often skipped.
