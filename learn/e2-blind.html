Unit E2 · the capstone

Where the Instrument
Is Blind

Every unit so far has shown what these reads can see. This one is the other half, and a course that never wrote it would be a sales brochure.

Here are two records. The second is a perfect deterministic function of the first — no noise, nothing hidden, every value of the second computable exactly from the first. Ask the instrument whether they are coupled.

B = |A|  →  coupling 0.000000, nothing resolved

That is what independent noise reads on the same instrument, on 58 of 60 draws. The relationship is total, and the read returns the same number it returns for two records with nothing between them at all.

That last row is a band, not a draw. It was one draw until 2026-08-28, reading an exact 0.000000 — and the very next draw from the same generator resolved at 0.06. Noise is resolved 2 times in 60 here, always small; a single sample of it is a coin toss reported as a constant.

3 of 10 perfect relationships are reported as indistinguishable from noise. Not weakly coupled — exactly zero, with nothing resolved above the floor.

And that zero is the honest result. A read that returned 0.04 here would be inviting you to believe in a faint connection between two records that are, in fact, perfectly connected. Being wrong quietly in the other direction would be worse.

The reads see the linear part of a relationship, and read zero when that part falls under the floor — however completely the relationship determines the answer. The correlation column above tracks the coupling column across every row.

This is the instrument's stated scope. These reads are linear-algebraic by construction, which is why they carry no trained model, need no fitting, and return in milliseconds. The blindness is what that buys, and a reader is owed the price alongside the goods.

Can the blind spot be moved?

If the reads see the linear part of a relationship, then the one thing that could change what they see is the thing that decides what counts as a coordinate — a lens. This course built lenses back in unit B1. So here is the whole table again, with a lens on the A side and nothing else altered.

It works. B = |A| reads 0.000000 under the plain identity — nothing — and 1.000000 through the |x| lens, which is more than ten times anything noise reached. The relationship was always total; the coordinates were wrong.

And the lens sells as well as buys. B = A stands at 1.000000 under the identity and falls to 0.000000 through the |x| lens, inside the noise band. Choosing a lens is choosing what to be blind to, and the choice is not free in one direction only.

It is also not a universal solvent. B = sin(3A) is below the band under every lens here, including the one built for the shape next to it. A lens matched to the wrong nonlinearity buys nothing.

Every cell in the table either clears its band or reads an exact zero. The instrument is not handing back small ambiguous numbers here — with one exception. B = A through the x² lens reads 0.064770 against a band ceiling of 0.063901: it clears by 1%. That cell is the one place where the verdict and the evidence part company, and nothing but a measured band would show it.

So the blind spot moves; it does not go away. To choose the lens that reveals a relationship you have to suspect its shape already — which is why the move on the next slide is still the one that works: plant the answer, and see whether it comes back.

Every one of those is a silent limit. None of them raises an error; each returns a perfectly well-formed answer to a question slightly different from the one you asked. That is what makes the list worth keeping, and what makes an exact zero valuable — it is the case where the instrument can tell.

How to know whether you are inside the blind spot: plant the answer and look for it. Build a record where you know what the relationship is, hand it to the read, and see whether it comes back. It is the same move unit A7 makes with the noise floor and unit A10 with the shape — and it is the cheapest check in this course.
