Both Sides of the Cut

Draw a line through a system and you have two sides. Measure how much each side carries and you get the same number twice — even when one side is twenty times the size of the other.

What a cut is here. A table of numbers has rows and columns. Read the rows as one side of the line and the columns as the other. Each side gets its own square summary table, one 2 by 2 and the other 64 by 64, and each has its own list of values to take an entropy of.

2 and 64, and the answer is the same to twelve decimals. The larger side has 64 values to the smaller side's 2, and it reports what the small side reports.

Unit A12 already does this arithmetic: square the values, turn them into shares, take −Σ p log2 p. Σ, said sigma, means add up the list. Here those three steps are applied to a cut.

The worst disagreement anywhere on this table is 4.44e-16. That is the smallest difference the arithmetic can express — on 2 of the five cuts the two sides come out exactly equal, with a difference of precisely zero.

The shape of the table has nothing to do with it. A 8 against 8 cut and a 2 against 64 cut both report one number from either side. What the two sides share is the list of values, and the next slide is that list.

A difference of 4.44e-16 means the two calculations went down completely different routes — one through a 2 by 2 table, the other through a 64 by 64 one — and landed on the same value.

What the two sides share

Take the 4 by 16 cut and open it up. Every table of numbers can be written as a list of σ values, said sigma, which say how much each independent pattern in it carries. This one has 4 of them:

The last two rows are the same numbers. The largest difference between them is 1.67e-16. Squaring the shared values gives exactly what the small side's own table reports, and the same squares come out of the large side's table too.

That is why both sides agree. They are reading one list. The two sides of a cut do not hold separate accounts to be reconciled; there is one set of values and each side sees all of it.

The squares add to one because the table was scaled that way, so they are already shares and the entropy can be taken straight off them.

The small side sets the limit

The large side's table is much bigger, so it has many more values in it. Count how many of them are above zero:

The count above zero is the smaller side, on every row. The 5 against 100 cut gives the large side 100 values in all, and 95 of them are exactly zero. Only 5 carry anything.

Making the far side larger adds nothing. Go from 100 columns to a thousand and the answer is still five values: the narrow side is the whole capacity of the cut. Everything the two sides can share has to pass through the smaller of them.

This is the bound the paper starts from, at a size you can check. A boundary of a given size carries a limited number of things, and enlarging what sits behind it changes that number by nothing. Units B6 and B7 measure what a limit like this does to a decay; this is where the limit comes from.

One arithmetic, two subjects. Unit A12 asks how many of a record's columns carry it. This page asks how much two sides of a cut share. The steps are identical and so is the meaning of the answer: 3.540525926 is an effective count, here of the patterns crossing the line.

What the paper takes from this. The entropy of the screen is the entropy of the cut, so the list of σ values is the whole of what the screen has to work with. Every read in this course — the resolution limit of unit B5, the shape constant of B6, the contrast of D5g — is worked out from that one list, which is why they can be compared with each other at all.

The measurement to keep: a 2-row side and a 64-row side, 0.894092706459 from each, and a difference of 2.22e-16.
